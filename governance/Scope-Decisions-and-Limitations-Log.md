## Standing principle: labeled assumptions vs. circular assumptions

A labeled assumption is valid when it fills in a fact genuinely outside your dataset — e.g., the AUD/hour cost-of-downtime benchmark, sourced from an external industry figure because AI4I has no cost data at all. A labeled assumption becomes illegitimate the moment it assumes the answer to the exact question your analysis is supposed to derive — e.g., assuming "X% of downtime is planned" in order to then report a planned-vs-unplanned split. That's not analysis, it's restating a guess. **Rule going forward: only assume facts external to the dataset. Never assume the answer you're trying to produce.** (Raised 20 Aug 2026, during business-question review.)

**Extension confirmed 25 Aug 2026:** this rule also draws the line between two kinds of "made up and disclosed" data, which look similar but aren't. Randomly assigning *identity* within a real constraint (e.g., which of 5 High-tier machines a High-type row belongs to) is legitimate scaffolding — the underlying fact (it's High-tier) is real, only the specific label is arbitrary, and no finding is computed from which specific machine it landed on. Randomly assigning a *value that would itself become the measured result* (a different repair time or repair cost per row, or a random date used to build a trend chart) is not legitimate, even with a disclosure attached — it would mean computing a "finding" out of numbers invented for that exact purpose. The fix for the second kind is always a single externally-sourced, cited benchmark applied uniformly (or by category), never a per-row random value.

**Second extension confirmed 25 Aug 2026, from the verification pass below:** a source being real is not the same as a claim about that source being accurate. Citing a genuine, findable paper is necessary but not sufficient — the specific number or range attributed to it has to be checked against what the source actually says, because a real citation can still misrepresent its own data (see the Ball/rod mill finding below). Verification is a required second pass, not an optional extra, on any benchmark headed into a deliverable.

## GOVERNANCE DECISION — sponsor/owner split, locked 1 September 2026

**Trigger:** `02-Stakeholder-Register.md` v1 §5 flag 1 raised an unresolved mismatch — the project charter named the Operations Superintendent as sole scope authority, but the decision-driven stakeholder pass showed the Maintenance Manager owns the budget, the KPI, and the primary decision on four of the six locked business questions.

**Decision: split the role rather than reassign it.**
- **Executive Sponsor — Operations Superintendent.** Commissions the engagement and holds its mandate; approves material scope variations; accepts at each phase gate and at close; escalation route for cross-functional blockers between Maintenance and Operations.
- **Accountable Business Owner — Maintenance Manager.** Owns the business outcome and the downtime/cost KPIs; signs off deliverable content (BRD, requirements, business case); assesses and recommends on scope variations; accountable for benefit realization after hand-over.

**The dividing line:** Sponsor approves *whether the work continues and within what boundary*; Business Owner approves *what the work says*.

**Rejected alternative:** reassigning sole sponsorship to the Maintenance Manager. Rejected because unplanned downtime lands on production, not maintenance — a maintenance-only mandate would leave the engagement without standing to ask Operations for equipment access, production data, or schedule impact, which is precisely the cross-functional friction a sponsor exists to resolve.

**Applied in:** `01-Project-Charter.md` v4 — §3 (two-tier change control), §4 (O0 and O6 sign-off), §5 (governance table), §11 (approval block), Appendix F (change log). `02-Stakeholder-Register.md` v2 — §4.1 (governance roles), §4.2 rows 1 and 7, §5 flag 1 marked resolved.

## Business questions — decision history from 20 Aug 2026 review

Drafted by Gagana, reviewed via critique-loop technique. Kept for reference on *why* each question landed where it did in the final set above.

### Kept as-is
- **"How much production time is being lost to unplanned breakdowns, and what does that cost?"** — Core research question. Directly answerable from AI4I's failure records once cleaned; drives the cost-of-downtime business case (Week 3, Day 13). The original "can we tell how it's affecting the overall business" question was folded into this one — same underlying question, avoid answering it twice under different labels. → Final set Tier 1, Q1.

### Reframed
- **Original: "How many breakdowns can be controlled?"** → **Reframed: "What proportion of failures fall into mechanically explainable/preventable modes (tool wear, heat dissipation, power, overstrain) versus the dataset's explicitly random, unpredictable failure mode (RNF)?"** Answerable directly from AI4I's own failure-mode taxonomy — no assumption needed, since RNF is defined by the dataset's creators as the non-predictable category. → Final set Tier 2, Q4.

- **Original: "How many breakdowns are expected vs. unexpected?"** → **Reframed: "Which failure modes show detectable precursor patterns in the sensor data before failure occurs (e.g., tool wear or torque trending toward known thresholds), versus which occur with no anticipatory signal?"** This avoids the circular-assumption trap entirely — it's answerable by looking at sensor trends in the data itself, not by guessing a planned/unplanned split. Bonus: this reframing sets up the business justification for the optional ML proof-of-concept module later — "some failures are anticipatable" is exactly the premise a predictive-maintenance recommendation rests on. → Final set Tier 3, Q6.

- **Original: "How do we avoid overstrain?"** → **Reframed: "What sensor conditions (torque, tool wear) are associated with overstrain failures, and at what threshold does risk increase?"** This is an analytical question the data can answer directly (AI4I's overstrain failures are mechanically tied to a torque × tool-wear relationship). "How do we avoid it" becomes the *recommendation* that follows from this analysis, not a question asked of the data directly. → Merged with the tool-wear-threshold question below; final set Tier 3, Q5.

### Dropped / flagged as a limitation
- **"How does weather affect these failures?"** — Not answerable with this dataset. AI4I's "air temperature" and "process temperature" columns are machine/process sensor readings from a controlled benchmark dataset, not external site weather data (rainfall, ambient conditions, dust events). Stretching these into a "weather" story would overclaim what the data supports. **Decision: drop from core scope; optionally name as a future-work limitation** ("a real engagement would pair this dataset with site weather data, which wasn't available here") rather than answering it with a misleading proxy. Not in the final set.

- **"How does failure rate/downtime trend over time (by month, season, etc.)?" (added 25 Aug 2026)** — Not answerable with this dataset, for the same structural reason as the repair-time gap below: AI4I has no timestamp or chronological ordering field at all. **Decision: do not fabricate dates to enable this.** Randomly assigning a date to each row and then charting a "trend" from those dates would mean reporting a pattern computed from noise you generated yourself — a direct violation of the labeled-assumption rule above, worse than the weather case because it would produce a chart that *looks* like a real finding. **Handling:** name the missing time dimension explicitly as a limitation in the README/data-lineage note ("AI4I has no timestamp field, so no trend, seasonality, or time-based analysis is possible with this dataset; a real engagement would use the CMMS's actual work-order dates"). A single illustrative date may appear on a one-off mockup (e.g., a sample work-order screenshot for the process map) purely as a presentation prop, never as a value feeding a chart or finding.

### New questions added 20 Aug 2026 (with rationale)

- **"Which asset type (AI4I's Type column: Low/Medium/High) has the highest failure rate and cost of downtime, and does that justify differentiated maintenance investment across asset tiers?"** — Why this helps: directly answerable from a real column already in the dataset, ties naturally to the asset criticality tiers you're already building into your synthetic asset register (Week 1, Day 1–2), and produces a segmentation insight — not just "downtime is bad" but "this specific tier deserves more investment," which is a sharper, more specific business case. → Final set Tier 2, Q3.

- **"What is the Pareto breakdown of failure modes — which one or two failure types account for the majority of downtime hours, and what would targeting just those achieve in cost savings?"** — Why this helps: this is your planned Week 2 Day 10 Pareto analysis, stated explicitly as a formal business question rather than left implicit. Naming it up front means your BRD, dashboard, and business case can all trace back to this exact question, strengthening your traceability matrix later. → Final set Tier 1, Q2.

- **"Is there a measurable tool-wear threshold beyond which failure probability rises sharply enough to justify a proactive replacement policy?"** — Why this helps: this converts a vague "reduce overstrain" ambition into a concrete, testable, specific recommendation ("replace tooling before X minutes of wear") — the kind of precise, defensible finding that reads as far more credible to a hiring manager than a general statement about improving maintenance practices. → Merged with the overstrain reframe above; final set Tier 3, Q5.

## Data quality findings — Machine failure vs. failure-mode logical consistency check (24 Aug 2026)

From the Week 2 Day 9 validation pass on the raw AI4I 2020 CSV (10,000 rows): checked whether the overall `Machine failure` flag agrees with the five individual failure-mode flags (TWF, HDF, PWF, OSF, RNF). Three distinct patterns turned up — treated as three different problems, not lumped together, because they have three different causes and three different fixes.

**Source-of-truth rule adopted:** `Machine failure` is the authoritative flag for anything measuring "did this asset go down" — total downtime hours, total cost, the Tier 1 diagnose-level metrics. The five mode flags are used only to *categorize* failures that `Machine failure` has already confirmed happened. This one rule resolves both real inconsistencies below cleanly.

**Finding 1 — 9 rows where `Machine failure = 1` but none of the five mode flags are set** (e.g., UDI 1438: `Machine failure = 1`, TWF/HDF/PWF/OSF/RNF all 0). Real failures by the source-of-truth rule, so they stay in downtime/cost totals — but not forced into one of the five named modes, since none of them claims it. **Decision:** add a sixth Pareto bucket, "Unclassified," for these 9 rows. Honest disclosure that 9 failures have no attributable mechanism, rather than inflating one of the five real categories to absorb them.

**Finding 2 — 18 rows where `RNF = 1` but `Machine failure = 0` (limitation — carry into the data-lineage note and README verbatim):**

> "18 rows show RNF triggered independently of the overall failure flag, consistent with RNF's documented ~0.1% random/independent design; excluded from failure counts per the Machine failure source-of-truth rule; wouldn't materially change conclusions given the small count."

Not treated as a data error. RNF is documented by the dataset's creators as an independent, randomly-triggered ~0.1%-probability flag, so a mismatch with the overall failure flag is expected generation behavior, not a defect to be silently patched. **Decision:** exclude these 18 rows from downtime totals and the failure-mode Pareto (since `Machine failure = 0` for them under the source-of-truth rule), and document the exclusion explicitly rather than dropping them without comment — a dashboard built naively off `Machine failure` alone would otherwise silently undercount RNF activity with no record of why.

**Finding 3 — 24 rows with more than one mode flag set simultaneously** (e.g., PWF and OSF triggered together on the same row). Not a data-quality issue — realistic cascading-failure behavior, not a defect. **Decision/rule for later:** when building the failure-mode Pareto chart (Week 2, Day 10), count by failure event (row), not by summing the five flag columns independently — summing the columns would double-count these 24 overlapping rows against the 339-row failure total.

**Why this belongs here:** this is a real, run-not-hypothetical logical-consistency check, and it's exactly the "data integrity rule" the Master Plan (Section 1.2) requires — every number traceable to the dataset, a cited source, or a labelled assumption. The Finding 2 disclosure in particular is a stronger, more specific limitation statement than most portfolio projects manage, because it names the exact row count, the exact mechanism (RNF's documented random/independent design), the exact handling rule applied, and an honest materiality call — not just "some rows didn't match."

## Data limitation — AI4I has no repair-time, repair-cost, or timestamp fields (24–25 Aug 2026)

**Finding (caught by Gagana while checking the dataset before Week 2 build):** AI4I 2020 is a single-snapshot dataset — each row is one sensor reading plus a failure flag, not a time-stamped failure event with a start and end. There is no field anywhere in the source data for time-to-repair, repair cost, labor, parts, or a date/timestamp of any kind. Practical effect: **MTTR (mean time to repair) cannot be computed from AI4I under any cleaning or re-skinning approach, and no chronological/trend analysis is possible either.** This is a correction to the Master Plan as originally written, not a new problem you created — the Week 2 Day 9/10 rows asked for "MTBF/MTTR calculations" as if both were directly derivable from the dataset, and that was inaccurate. Catching this now, before the Week 3 business-case build, is exactly the kind of catch worth logging as a quality signal in the Efficiency Tracker (task: data-scope verification before build; AI role: none — this was found independently; quality signal: prevented building the Week 3 cost model on an uncomputable metric).

**Decision — resolved under the existing labeled-assumption rule, not an exception to it:** Two additional external, cited benchmarks are needed alongside the AUD/hour downtime-cost figure already planned in Section 1.2 of the Master Plan:
1. **Average repair time per failure event** (an MTTR proxy) — sourced from an external mining/industrial-maintenance reliability benchmark. Use a per-failure-mode or per-asset-tier figure if a defensible public source supports that granularity; otherwise a single overall average, clearly flagged as such.
2. **Average repair cost per failure event** (labor + parts) — same sourcing approach.

Both qualify as legitimate labeled assumptions under the standing principle above: they are facts genuinely external to the dataset, in the same category as the AUD/hour figure — not assumptions about the answer the analysis is meant to derive. What stays genuinely data-derived: failure counts, failure rate, the failure-mode Pareto, and asset-type/tier segmentation — all computed directly from AI4I. What becomes assumption-derived: the absolute hours-lost and dollar figures, which apply these external multipliers uniformly across that real, data-derived segmentation. **Tier 1 Q1 stays in the final question set as written**, but every chart/table that uses it must visibly separate the data-derived count from the assumption-derived dollar/hours figure, per the Master Plan's existing three-tag data integrity rule (dataset / cited source / labelled assumption) — not blend them into one number that reads as if AI4I produced it directly.

**Sourcing prompt drafted 25 Aug 2026:** a citation-discipline prompt was written to run in a **fresh chat** (deliberately, same fresh-context technique as the BRD red-team step) asking for real, cited external benchmarks for average repair time and average repair cost per failure event, broken down by failure mode/asset tier where a defensible source supports it, with explicit instructions not to fabricate a source and to disclose plainly if only a single overall figure is available. Technique: structured output + citation-discipline / source-vetting prompting, the same family as the AUD/hour sourcing task and the rejected-dataset vetting note — this run additionally used iterative multi-source triangulation (search → fetch primary document → verify the number is actually in the document, not just in a marketing blog restating it) rather than accepting the first plausible-looking search hit.

## Limitation — sub-threshold stoppages are structurally invisible to any work-order-based analysis, including this project's (raised 3 September 2026)

**Finding, from the Reliability Engineer interview (Q11, unprompted):** on a real mine site, short stoppages under roughly 15 minutes — a conveyor tripping and resetting, an excavator stalling and restarting, a crusher choking and self-clearing — never generate a work order at all. They surface only in an operator's shift-handover log, if anywhere, and that log doesn't connect to the CMMS. An asset can be tripping out repeatedly within a single shift and show a completely clean maintenance history, because nothing about a sub-threshold stop ever becomes a logged, queryable record.

**Why this belongs here rather than only in the transcript:** this isn't a complaint about one company's sloppy data entry — it's a structural property of *any* analysis built on logged breakdown/work-order events, including this project's. AI4I 2020 is itself a snapshot of logged failure events; it has no mechanism to represent a sub-threshold stoppage that never became a "failure" in the first place. So this project's Pareto analysis, failure-rate segmentation, and precursor-pattern work (Q2, Q4, Q6) are all implicitly scoped to failures that cross a reporting threshold — real, useful findings, but bounded ones. A genuinely high-frequency, low-severity problem (exactly the "death by a thousand cuts" pattern the Reliability Engineer separately named for conveyor idlers) could be invisible to this entire methodology even if it's a bigger true cost than anything the analysis surfaces.

**Decision: name this explicitly as a scope boundary, not a data-cleaning defect to fix.** Nothing about how AI4I is cleaned or re-skinned can address this — there is no sub-threshold event data to recover, synthetic or otherwise, without fabricating it, which the labeled-assumption rule above already rules out. Add a line to the eventual README/data-lineage note and the BRD's limitations section along these lines: "This analysis is bounded to logged, above-threshold breakdown events. Recurring sub-threshold stoppages (brief trips, stalls, self-clearing jams) are a known blind spot of any work-order-based dataset, real or synthetic, and are not captured here — a real engagement would need shift-log or SCADA-level event data to see this category of loss at all." This is a stronger, more specific limitation than most portfolio projects manage, in the same family as the earlier RNF and timestamp disclosures — caught through an interview technique, not through auditing the dataset itself, which is worth naming as a distinct win for the elicitation phase specifically.

**Cross-reference:** the same interview independently raised a related, second-order version of this gap — verbal shift-handover warnings ("keep an eye on this") that never get written down anywhere searchable. Same underlying theme (information exists but never becomes data); not a separate limitation, just the human-communication half of the same structural gap.

## DECISION — Ancillary-tier repair-time aggregation, resolved 8 September 2026: Option C, no figure

**Trigger:** the open item immediately above. Three options were weighed for turning the Idaho National Laboratory proxy's three equipment-specific figures (Pump 9.6 hrs / Valve 11.6 hrs / Motor 6.9 hrs) into one usable Ancillary-tier number: **(A)** blend the three into a single average and apply it to all 29 Ancillary assets; **(B)** apply the closest-matching figure only to the subset of Ancillary assets that are actually pumps/valves/motors, leaving the rest (graders, dozers, water carts, generators, compressors, feeders, service trucks, light vehicles) with no figure; **(C)** apply no repair-time figure to the Ancillary tier at all, reporting it by failure frequency only — the same treatment already locked for the High tier under the 1 Sept 2026 Option B decision.

**Decision: Option C.** Recommended by Claude at Gagana's request, on three grounds: (1) **consistency with existing precedent** — the High tier already reports by frequency only for the same underlying reason (a source that covers only part of the tier isn't a defensible stand-in for the whole tier), so treating Ancillary the same way applies one coherent rule across the whole fleet rather than a different improvised fix per tier; (2) **defensibility under questioning** — Option A silently overreaches the source (applying pump/valve/motor data to a grader or a light vehicle) and Option B creates an awkward, hard-to-explain split where roughly a third of the tier gets a number and two-thirds don't, for reasons a reader would have to be walked through; Option C requires no such explanation beyond "the source doesn't cover this equipment class, so we didn't force it"; (3) **it surfaces a more accurate coverage claim** — see the correction below.

**Correction this surfaces, applied to the 1 September 2026 Option B entry above:** that entry's closing line — "real, sourced dollar figures still cover 43 of the fleet's 48 assets" — assumed the Ancillary tier's proxy figures were usable across all 29 Ancillary assets. With this decision, they aren't used at all. Actual real-repair-time coverage is the **Mobile fleet's 14 assets only** (the Medium tier), not 43. The correction has been added inline to that entry rather than silently edited out, consistent with this project's error-disclosure convention (see the ball/rod-mill verification-pass entry above for the same convention applied to a Claude-sourced citation error).

**What this does and doesn't affect:** only the Ancillary-tier (Low-criticality) downtime-hours query in Day 9's SQL work. Does not affect Mobile fleet (Medium tier, fully sourced) or High tier (already frequency-only under the existing Option B decision). Does not reduce the six locked business questions' answerability — Q1 and Q3 already carried a disclosed Medium/Low-tier-costed, High-tier-frequency-only caveat; that caveat now reads as Medium-tier-costed, High-and-Low-tier-frequency-only, which is a more accurate statement of the same caveat, not a new limitation.

**Applied in:** `05-Data-Dictionary.md` §3.1–3.2 (updated 8 Sept 2026); this entry and the correction note on the 1 Sept 2026 Option B entry above.
