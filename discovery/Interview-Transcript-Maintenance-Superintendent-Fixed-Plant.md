## Transcript

**1. Walk me through how you decide where your crew's attention goes in a typical week across the crusher, mill, conveyors, and thickener — what tells you something needs looking at?**

Honestly? Half of it's the crew just knowing the gear — you get a feel for what's about to give you grief. But the real triggers are: operators radioing in that something's making a noise it shouldn't, or the control room flagging an alarm or a trend that's out of whack — amps creeping up on the mill motor, that sort of thing. Then there's whatever's sitting on the outstanding work order list from the last shift handover, and whatever failed last time that we half-arsed a fix on because we didn't have the part or the downtime window.

Crusher and mill get priority pretty much by default because if they're down, nothing downstream is running — conveyors and thickener you can nurse along a bit more, patch something up, keep it going till a planned window. So realistically I'm triaging: what's actually stopped production right now, what's about to, and what can wait for the next shut.

If I'm honest, a lot of it's reactive. We've got a preventative maintenance schedule on paper, but when you're short-handed — which is often — that's the first thing that slips, and then you're just chasing whatever breaks next. That's part of the problem you lot are meant to be looking at, isn't it.

**2. When something on your gear breaks down, how well does the current system actually capture what happened, how long it was down, and what it cost to fix?**

Not well, mate, if I'm being straight with you. We've got the CMMS — the work order system — and blokes are supposed to log a work order when they fix something, put in a bit of a description, close it out. That part mostly happens, eventually. Where it falls down is the actual downtime numbers.

Production log the stoppage on their end with their own start and finish times, and we log the work order with ours, and half the time they don't match up because nobody's out there synchronising a stopwatch — it's "yeah, roughly from smoko till we got it running again." So if you go pull downtime hours off two different systems for the same event you'll get two different answers, and neither of them's gospel.

Cause codes are the other issue. There's a dropdown in the CMMS for failure reason and it's got about forty options, half of which don't really fit what actually happened, so guys just pick whatever's closest or chuck it under "other" and write the real story in a free-text comment — if they write anything at all. So when someone tries to run a report on "what's our top failure mode on the crusher," you're at the mercy of whether somebody typed a decent note that day.

Cost — nah, we don't really track that at the repair level. Parts get drawn from the store against a work order number so there's a paper trail if you go digging, but labour hours, we're not logging actual time spent per job, it's more like whoever was rostered on that shift. So if you want a proper cost-per-breakdown figure, it doesn't exist today — you'd have to go build it from a few different places and make some assumptions.

**3. Here's something worth reacting to: this project couldn't find trustworthy repair-time or repair-cost numbers for four of your five asset types, so the plan is to report those by how often they fail, not by dollars — and to say plainly why. Does that feel useful to you, or does it miss what you'd actually want to know?**

Yeah — nah, that's fair, and honestly it's more honest than what we've got now, which is nothing, or numbers people quote like they're solid when they're really just vibes off a spreadsheet somebody built once and never updated.

Frequency's useful to me. If you can tell me the thickener's tripped out eight times this quarter and the conveyor's only tripped twice, that tells me where to point the crew even without a dollar figure attached. That's actually closer to how I already think about it day to day — I'm not sitting there doing NPV on a bearing failure, I'm going "that thing keeps stopping, sort it."

Where it bites us is when it goes upstairs. Management wants a dollar figure because that's the language that gets a shutdown approved or a spare parts budget signed off. If the report just says "crusher failed twelve times" and no cost attached, I can already picture some bean counter going "so what, is that bad?" without something to compare it against — lost production hours, tonnes not milled, whatever. So if you're going down the frequency road, at least pair it with downtime hours, not just a count of events. Twelve five-minute nuisance trips is a very different story to twelve four-hour ones, and just counting "failures" flattens that out.

And be upfront in the report about why cost isn't there — like you said. Because if you quietly leave it out, someone in a meeting is going to ask "what did that cost us" and it looks like the analysis is incomplete rather than the data genuinely not existing. Say it straight: the cost data doesn't exist in a trustworthy form today, here's frequency and downtime instead, here's what it'd take to get proper cost numbers if leadership wants to invest in that later.

**4. Why do you think repair time doesn't get captured well for this equipment — is it that breakdowns are rare, your crew gets pulled onto other jobs mid-repair, or something else?**

Bit of all that, honestly, but if you want the main one — it's that nobody's actually timing the job while it's happening. You've got a bloke elbow-deep in a gearbox, he's not stopping to write down "started at 14:32." He fixes it, gets it running again 'cause that's what everyone wants, and only after the fact does he go log a work order — sometimes same day, sometimes it's end of shift, sometimes honestly it's a couple days later if it's been flat out. So the times that go in are reconstructed from memory, not recorded live.

Getting pulled off mid-job is real too. Say we're halfway through a conveyor idler change and the mill trips — that crew's now split, or fully redirected, and that work order just sits open while they're off doing the higher priority thing. When they come back and close it out, the "duration" on that work order includes all the dead time they weren't even touching it, but the system's got no way of showing that — it just sees open-to-close as one block of time. So the number's technically there but it's rubbish, it overstates how long the actual repair took.

Rare events is part of it for some gear, yeah — thickener doesn't fail often so when it does, whoever's writing it up isn't in the habit of doing it properly, versus something like conveyor idlers where the crew's basically got the paperwork muscle memory down 'cause they do it every other week.

And I'll say it plain — there's no real accountability pressure on getting the time right. Nobody's ever pulled a bloke up and said "mate, this duration doesn't add up." So it's never been a priority to get it accurate, it's just a box that needs something in it to close the work order.

**5. If you had to name your own worst offender — the crusher, the mill, a particular conveyor, or the thickener — what would it be, and how sure are you?**

If you're asking gut feel — crusher, no question. Specifically the primary jaw, not so much the secondary. It's old, it's had a hard life, and it feels like every few weeks we're up there for something, liners, toggle plate, one bearing dramas last year that took us out for the better part of a shift. It's the one where the boys groan when the radio goes off.

But how sure am I — put it this way, I wouldn't bet my job on that being what the numbers actually say, because like I just told you, our data's not good enough to prove it. It might just be that the crusher failures are the noisiest and most disruptive so they stick in my head, whereas something like the conveyors might genuinely be failing more often but each one's a quick fix so nobody remembers it — idler roller, five minutes, back running, nobody writes a war story about it. Frequency without someone actually crunching it properly could easily surprise me.

I'll tell you who'll argue with me on this one too — talk to the reliability engineer, he's convinced it's actually the mill that's bleeding us the most hours, not event count but total time down, 'cause when the mill goes it's a proper job, not a quick spanner turn. He might be right, I honestly don't know, we've never sat down and actually looked at it side by side with real numbers. That's sort of the whole problem in a nutshell, isn't it — everyone's got their own asset they reckon is the worst and nobody's got the data to settle it.

## Key findings extracted (BA synthesis)

**1. Direct, operational validation of the Option B decision — with one important refinement.** Kev independently prefers a disclosed frequency-only gap over quoted-but-untrustworthy numbers ("more honest than what we've got now"), and says frequency already matches how he thinks day to day. But he adds a specific requirement: frequency needs to be paired with downtime *hours*, not reported as a bare event count, because a dozen five-minute nuisance trips and a dozen four-hour failures are very different stories that a plain count flattens. **This can't actually be delivered for fixed plant** — downtime hours is exactly the figure Option B exists because it isn't trustworthy for four of the five fixed-plant asset types (Scope-Decisions-and-Limitations-Log, 25 Aug 2026). So his feedback doesn't change the decision — it strengthens the case for naming the CMMS repair-duration-capture gap as its own actionable recommendation (see finding 3), since even the frontline consumer of this data wants more than frequency alone and the project can't fully give it to him yet.

**2. A third, independent, operational account of the same repair-cost/repair-time data gap.** The Financial Controller (finance-system view) and, earlier, the Reliability Engineer and Maintenance Manager (technical/management view) all separately described this gap; Kev now confirms it from the frontline execution side: two different downtime clocks (production's stopwatch vs. maintenance's work order) that routinely disagree, a ~40-option cause-code dropdown where half the options don't fit so people default to "other," and no per-job labour-hour logging at all — parts have a paper trail via the store, labour cost doesn't exist below "whoever was rostered." Four stakeholders, four vantage points, one consistent picture.

**3. The single most useful new finding: a specific, named root cause for why fixed-plant repair-duration data is bad, not just a general observation that it is.** Jobs aren't timed live — a tradesman mid-repair isn't logging a start time, so durations are reconstructed from memory, sometimes days later. Crews get pulled off a job mid-repair onto a higher-priority failure, and the system has no way to exclude that dead time from the work order's recorded duration, so it silently overstates real repair time. Rare-event assets (thickener) lack the "paperwork muscle memory" that frequent-event ones (conveyor idlers) have built up. And there's no review process that ever catches an implausible duration. This converts the Financial Controller's recommended finding ("current CMMS close-out doesn't reliably capture repair duration") from a vague statement into a specific, mechanism-level one — directly usable in the BRD's limitations section and as the justification for a concrete functional requirement (e.g., live start/stop capture against a work order, or flagging jobs that were reassigned mid-repair so duration can be corrected).

**4. A second independent confirmation of the same cognitive bias the Operations Manager named about himself.** Kev's own gut pick (the primary jaw crusher) comes with an unprompted, self-aware caveat: dramatic, disruptive failures stick in memory, while frequent-but-quick fixes (a five-minute idler swap) don't, so his instinct could easily be wrong once someone actually counts properly. This is close to word-for-word the same bias the Mining/Operations Manager flagged about his own downtime estimate (that transcript, Q5: "the breakdowns I remember are the dramatic ones... what I'm probably missing is death by a thousand cuts"). Two senior stakeholders, independently, naming the same specific bias in their own reasoning — strong, reusable material for the BRD's problem statement: this isn't just "the CMMS is messy," it's "human memory for failure patterns is systematically biased toward dramatic, infrequent events over frequent, minor ones," which is exactly what a proper Pareto analysis (Q2) is built to correct for.

**5. A genuinely quotable illustration of why this project needs to exist at all.** Kev names the Reliability Engineer as having a different, competing guess for the worst offender (the mill, by total downtime hours, not event count) and says plainly: "we've never sat down and actually looked at it side by side with real numbers... everyone's got their own asset they reckon is the worst and nobody's got the data to settle it." Two experienced, senior people, confidently disagreeing, with no way to resolve it today — a clean, concrete, human illustration of the exact problem the project's failure-mode Pareto (Q2) and asset-tier segmentation (Q3) exist to solve. Strong candidate for the BRD's opening problem statement or the video script's hook.

**6. Light corroboration of the fixed-plant criticality logic already locked in the project.** Kev's own reasoning for why crusher and mill get default priority — "if they're down, nothing downstream is running" — independently matches the consequence-of-failure logic already used to assign fixed-plant assets to the High criticality tier (Scope-Decisions-and-Limitations-Log, 24–25 Aug 2026 entry). Not a new decision, just a good, real-sounding confirmation that the existing tiering logic reflects how the equipment is actually thought about operationally.
