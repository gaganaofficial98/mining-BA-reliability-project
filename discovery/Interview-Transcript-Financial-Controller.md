**1. Walk me through how a maintenance or downtime cost figure actually gets from the shop floor into your monthly cost report — what do you receive, from whom, and how much of it do you take at face value versus check yourself?**

*(leans back, laptop still closed)* Okay, so — the honest answer is it comes from about four different places and I trust them to wildly different degrees.

The bulk of the dollar figure — actual maintenance cost — comes out of the ERP, out of the finance system. Purchase orders, invoices coded to maintenance cost centres, labour hours from the payroll interface for the trades. That part I don't lose sleep over. If a contractor invoice says $40,000 for a conveyor gearbox replacement, that's a real number, it's been through AP, it's coded against an asset or a work order number. That's the bit I'd call "true" cost.

Where it gets murky is the downtime side — the hours lost, and more specifically what caused them. That comes from Dale's team, out of the CMMS, or off the shift log if the CMMS entry hasn't been done yet — which, let's be honest, happens more than it should. And that's where I start asking questions. Because a work order in the CMMS might say "3.5 hours, mechanical" and I've got no way of independently verifying that 3.5 hours against anything except... I suppose the shift supervisor's handover notes, if I go looking for them, which I don't always have time to do.

Then there's a third input, which is the bit that actually worries me most for a business case — somebody, usually in Operations, translates downtime hours into a dollar figure using a "cost of downtime per hour" rate. And every time I've asked where that rate comes from, I get a slightly different answer. Sometimes it's tonnes-not-mined times price, sometimes it's a blended figure someone built in Excel two years ago and nobody's revisited. I take that number and I use it because I have to put something in the report, but if you asked me to defend it in front of the GM I'd be sweating a bit.

So to your actual question — from whom, and how much do I check. Finance-system cost data: I take at close to face value, spot-check maybe one in twenty entries against source docs during month-end. CMMS downtime hours and cause codes: I don't check those at all, really, I don't have the access or the time, I just consume whatever's been entered by the time I pull the report — which means if a work order's still open or the cause code's wrong, that error just... flows straight into my report and I don't know it's there. And the dollar-per-hour downtime rate — that one I've pushed back on a couple of times and been told it's "close enough for reporting purposes," which is not a sentence that fills me with confidence when it's going into something head office might use to approve or reject capital spend.

The pain for me, sitting where I sit — I'm the one who signs the report. My name's effectively on that dollar figure. And I'm assembling it from a system I control tightly and two inputs I basically have to take on trust from other teams, on a deadline, without a clean audit trail back to source. If someone at head office ever asked me "walk me through exactly how you got to $2.1 million in downtime cost this quarter," I could show them the finance side no problem. The CMMS-to-dollars bit — I'd be doing some fast talking.

Bigger than everything else combined for that asset class. And when one number's doing that much of the lifting in a report that's going up to the GM, I get twitchy. That's just — that's the job, really, anything that disproportionate gets a second look from me.

So what did I actually do. I didn't have a clean way to verify it myself, so I rang Dale. And to be fair to him he chased it up quickly. Turned out — from what came back to me, and this is secondhand, I wasn't in the room — that part of that window was actually a planned inspection that ran long and got tangled up with the actual failure in the one work order, because whoever logged it just used a single WO for the whole outage instead of splitting planned versus unplanned. Which, fine, we adjusted it. But here's my actual gripe with the process, and this is the bit that matters for what you're doing — I only caught that because the number was big enough to be visually obvious sitting in a spreadsheet. I don't have the time or, frankly, the technical grounding to interrogate every downtime entry against the shift log. So my real doubt isn't about that one crusher event — it's that I've got no idea how many smaller versions of that same mistake are sitting in every report I've signed off on that I never happened to catch. There's no reconciliation step, there's no flag that says "this WO spans a planned and unplanned event, please split it." It just relies on someone entering it right the first time, or on me getting lucky enough to notice something looks off.

**3. When Maintenance or Operations asks you to approve spend based on a downtime or breakdown-cost argument, what's missing from that request more often than not?**

*(exhales, like this one's got a bit of history behind it)* Oh — where do I start. Honestly, more often than not what's missing is the working. I get handed a business case — "we want to replace the thickener rake mechanism, it's costing us X in downtime" — and there's a number, a big confident number, and there's a recommendation, but there's no path between the two that I can actually follow.

Specifically, a few things, every time:

One — there's no baseline. Nobody's shown me "here's what this asset has cost us in downtime over the last twelve or twenty-four months, consistently measured," it's usually one or two headline incidents that get pulled out because they're memorable and dramatic, and then that gets extrapolated into an annual figure. And extrapolating from your worst month is not the same as an actual trend, but that's often what's presented to me as if it is.

Two — the assumptions behind the dollar rate are never stated. I've said this before to Dale, actually, half-jokingly — every business case just says "at $X per hour of downtime" like that number fell from the sky. Nobody shows me is that the marginal cost of lost production, is it including fixed costs that would be there anyway, is it the same rate we used in last quarter's business case for a completely different part of the plant. I've seen two different capex requests in the same year use two different downtime rates for the same asset class and nobody blinked.

Three, and this is the one that actually annoys me most — there's rarely anything that separates "this will reduce downtime" from "this will reduce the cost of downtime we've experienced." Those aren't the same thing. Somebody might genuinely fix a real reliability problem and the number of hours goes down, but if they haven't also shown me why the current figure is right, I can't tell if the projected saving is real or if we're just going to end up chasing a bad baseline down to a slightly-less-bad baseline.

And four — and I get why this happens, I'm not trying to be precious about it — there's no acknowledgement of uncertainty anywhere. Everything's presented as a hard number. Nobody says "this is our best estimate, confidence is moderate, here's the range." If someone came to me with "downtime cost is between $1.8 and $2.4 million, our best estimate's $2.1," I'd trust that more than someone telling me it's exactly $2,147,000, because I know for a fact nobody's tracking it that precisely. The false precision is honestly the thing that erodes my confidence fastest — it tells me either they don't understand where the number came from, or they're hoping I won't ask.

So when it lands on my desk, my job basically becomes doing the digging that should've been done before it got to me — going back to Dale's team, or over to Operations, asking for the work order numbers behind the headline figure, trying to see if the rate's been applied consistently. Some months I've got time to do that properly. Some months, honestly, I don't, and things get signed off on a number I'm not a hundred percent comfortable with, because the deadline's the deadline. That's not a great place for a Financial Controller to be, if I'm honest with you.

**4. If someone showed you a report that said "unplanned downtime cost the site $X this month," what would you need to see before you'd let that number anywhere near a board pack or a budget submission?**

*(finally opens the laptop, more as a prop than to look at anything)* Right, this is a good one to ask me directly, because I've actually thought about what I'd want if I were designing it myself, even though nobody's asked me to.

First thing — I need to be able to trace it back. Not summarised, not aggregated straight into a headline figure, I mean I want to be able to click or drill or however it's presented and get down to the actual work orders and the actual hours that make up that number. If I can't get from "$2.1 million" down to "here are the fourteen events that sum to that," I'm not signing it. That's non-negotiable for me, that's basically the whole job.

Second — the methodology has to be documented and consistent, and I mean genuinely consistent, not "consistent this quarter." What's the downtime rate, per asset or per area, where did it come from, when was it last reviewed, and is it the same rate that was used last month and the month before. If the rate changes, I want to know why and I want that noted, not just quietly baked into a new number.

Third, and this is the crusher example again really — I need planned versus unplanned separated out properly, and I need to trust that separation happened at the point of data entry, not patched up after the fact because I happened to notice something looked big. So some way of knowing the data's actually been categorised correctly before it gets to me, not just after.

Fourth — I want it broken down by asset and by cause, not just one lump sum for the site. Because a board or a budget committee is going to ask "what's driving this," and if the answer is "unplanned downtime, various," that's useless. I need to be able to say it's the crusher, it's bearing failures specifically, it's happened four times this year, here's the trend.

Fifth — and this is maybe the biggest one for me personally — I want some honesty about confidence. If half the figure's built on solid finance-system invoice data and half's built on an estimated rate applied to CMMS hours that might have data quality issues, tell me that. Flag which bits are hard and which bits are soft. I don't need everything to be perfect, I need to know what I'm actually vouching for when I put my name on it.

And sixth, practically — I want it to tie back cleanly to what's already in the finance system, so I'm not running two parallel versions of the truth. If Operations' downtime dollar figure and my actual maintenance spend figure can't be reconciled against each other in some sensible way, that's a problem, because eventually someone at head office is going to put those two numbers side by side and ask why they don't match up, and "well, they're measuring different things" is a conversation I'd rather not be having in front of the GM.

Basically — traceable, consistently defined, properly categorised, broken down sensibly, honest about its own limitations, and reconcilable with the numbers I already own. Give me that and I'll sign it without losing sleep. Give me what I've been getting, and I'll sign it anyway because I usually have to, but I won't be happy about it, and I'll be the one in the room asking the awkward question if it ever gets challenged.

**5. Here's something worth reacting to directly: this project looked hard for real, published data on maintenance repair cost — labor and parts, not lost production value — for mining equipment, and mining-specific or general industrial, it doesn't exist anywhere that's publicly verifiable. So the business case is being built entirely on downtime cost — the value of lost production per hour — not repair cost. Does that trade-off sit alright with you, or does dropping repair cost entirely weaken the number more than it looks like on paper?**

*(sits back, and this one clearly gets her attention — she straightens up a bit)* Okay, hang on, let me push back on the framing a little before I answer, because I think there's a wrinkle in it. When you say published data on repair cost doesn't exist anywhere — are you talking about external benchmarks, industry-wide numbers? Because if that's what you mean, sure, I'd believe that, nobody's going to publish "here's what a mill bearing job costs across the WA mining sector," that's commercially sensitive, every site's fleet and contracts are different. But we don't need someone else's published number for that. We have our own. That's literally the one part of this whole conversation I told you I actually trust — labour hours, parts, contractor invoices, all sitting in the ERP, coded to work orders. That's real, auditable repair cost, ours, already. So if the reason it's being dropped is "it's not out there publicly," that doesn't sit right with me, because it was never meant to be a benchmarking exercise, was it — it's our number, about our assets.

Now, if what's actually happened is a decision to scope it out of the business case entirely and lean only on downtime — lost production value — then my honest reaction is: that makes me more nervous, not less, and here's why. Downtime cost, the dollar-per-hour rate, is the softest, least-defensible number in the whole chain — I said that earlier, I've watched two business cases use two different rates for the same asset in the same year. Repair cost is the one number in this entire exercise that's actually hard, traceable, sitting in a system I control. If you drop that and build the whole case on the number I trust least, you've taken the one bit of solid ground you had and left it out, and built the tower on the sand instead. That's the trade-off, put bluntly.

Does it weaken the number? It depends what the number's for. If the argument is purely "look how much production we're losing, that alone justifies investment" — fine, that can stand on its own, and honestly in mining the production value dwarfs the repair bill most of the time, a few hours of crusher downtime is worth more than the bearing that caused it, so directionally it's not dishonest to lead with downtime. But if this is going into a proper business case with a return-on-investment argument, and somebody at head office who thinks the way I do looks at it, the first thing they'll ask is "so if we fix this, does our maintenance spend go down too, or does it stay the same and we just get more uptime?" And if the case is silent on that because repair cost was scoped out, it looks like nobody thought about it — even if the real reason is a completely defensible data-quality call. That's the actual risk. Not that the number's wrong, but that it looks incomplete to someone whose job, like mine, is to find the gap.

So what I'd actually want, if I were signing off on this — keep downtime cost as the headline driver, that's fine, that's genuinely the bigger number and the more compelling one. But don't drop repair cost silently. Either bring it in as a supporting data point — "and separately, here's the trend in reactive repair spend on this asset over the same period, which is also worth watching" — using our own actual invoice data, which costs you nothing extra to pull because I've already got it clean. Or, if it's genuinely out of scope, say so explicitly in the case itself: "this analysis does not include potential reductions in maintenance labour and parts cost, which represents additional unquantified upside." One sentence. That way it reads as a deliberate, disclosed scoping decision instead of something I have to go digging for and then ask about in the meeting, which — you can probably tell by now — is exactly the kind of thing I'd ask about in the meeting.

**6. For the site's fixed plant — crusher, mill, conveyors, thickener — there's real failure-frequency data but no trustworthy repair-time or repair-cost figure for four of the five asset types, so the plan is to report those in frequency terms only, with no dollar figure attached, rather than force a number that isn't defensible. As the person who has to defend the business case if it's picked apart, does that read as an honest gap to you, or does a business case with a hole in it like that lose credibility the moment someone notices?**

*(nods slowly, actually looks approving before she even says anything)* Right — my honest answer is I like that a lot more than the alternative, and I want to say that plainly before I get to the "but." Because the alternative is somebody backing into a number using the crusher's rate or an average across the fleet just so every row in the table has a dollar figure in it, and that is exactly the kind of thing that, if I ever had to defend it, I couldn't. A blank cell with a documented reason behind it is something I can stand behind. A confident-looking wrong number is something that eventually gets me in front of the GM explaining myself, and I've been there, I don't want to go back.

So — honest gap, yes. That's the right instinct and it's consistent with what I said earlier, I'd rather see a disclosed limitation than false precision.

Now the "but," because there is one, and it's about presentation, not about the underlying decision. A business case that has one asset costed properly and four reported as frequency-only, sitting next to each other in the same document — that's going to draw attention, and not necessarily the good kind. Somebody skimming it, especially somebody who isn't across the nuance, is going to look at four blank dollar columns and their gut reaction isn't "ah, rigorous data discipline," it's "why couldn't they work this out," and that reads as incompleteness before anyone's actually engaged with the reasoning. So the gap itself doesn't lose you credibility — the unexplained gap does. If it's just presented as a hole, someone will pick at it, and if they pick at it and there's no answer sitting right there in the document, that's when it looks bad. If instead every one of those frequency-only rows has a one-line note — something like "repair-time not reliably captured in CMMS for this asset class, cost not quantified to avoid overstating confidence" — then I've got an answer ready before the question's even asked. That's the difference between a hole and a disclosed boundary.

Second thing — I'd actually turn it around and make it work for the case rather than just being a caveat you're managing. The fact that you can't get a trustworthy repair-time figure for four out of five fixed plant asset types is itself a finding. That's a process gap, that's something worth a line of its own — "current work order close-out doesn't reliably capture repair duration, which limits our ability to cost downtime by cause for these assets" — because that becomes a recommendation, not just an excuse. It tells whoever's reading it that the project's already found something actionable, which is a stronger position than quietly hiding the gap and hoping nobody counts the blank cells.

Third — and this is a smaller point but it'll bite you if you don't think about it — I'd want that frequency-only treatment to be consistent with what shows up in my monthly cost report for the same assets. If your business case says "no defensible dollar figure for mill downtime" and then two months later my report has a mill downtime dollar figure sitting in it because I had to put something in the field, someone's going to notice the mismatch and ask which one's right, and neither of us wants that conversation. So if this is the standard you're applying, I'd genuinely want to know about it, because it might change how I flag those same lines in my own reporting.

So — no, it doesn't lose credibility the moment someone notices, as long as someone noticing is expected and already answered on the page. It loses credibility if someone notices and there's nothing there to catch them. That's really the whole difference.

**7. If maintenance came to you wanting a bigger planned-downtime allowance on the basis of "this asset's failed three times this quarter, here's what it's cost us" — where does your read of that request actually diverge from how Operations would look at the same numbers?**

*(picks up a pen, turns it over once — this is clearly a familiar dynamic to her)* That's a good one to ask, because it's not really about the number at all, it's about what each of us is actually optimising for when we look at the same three data points.

Maintenance — Dale's team — when they bring me "it's failed three times this quarter, here's what it's cost us," they're thinking in terms of asset condition and access. Their argument is essentially: give us the planned hours now, in a controlled way, on our schedule, and we avoid the uncontrolled version happening again at 2am on a Sunday. That's a reliability argument, and honestly it's usually a reasonable one on its face.

Operations looks at the exact same three numbers and sees something completely different — they see three guaranteed slabs of tonnes they didn't hit, against a target they're being measured on right now, this quarter. And when Maintenance comes back and says "so give us more planned downtime," what Operations hears is "give up more guaranteed, scheduled tonnes, in exchange for a promise that the unplanned losses will be smaller." And that's a much harder trade to say yes to than it sounds, because a planned outage is a certain loss you can see coming, and the unplanned failures it's supposedly preventing are still, in their mind, hypothetical — maybe it happens again, maybe it doesn't, maybe it's four times next quarter or maybe it's zero. People, and I include Operations in this, weight a certain loss today more heavily than an uncertain saving later, even when the maths says they shouldn't. So where Maintenance sees "we're preventing pain," Operations often sees "you're asking me to pre-pay for pain I haven't had yet."

Where I diverge from both of them, honestly, is that I don't fully trust either framing on its own. My first question isn't "do we say yes" — it's "is three failures this quarter actually a signal, or is it three failures that happened to land in the same quarter and it's noise." Because if I approve a permanent increase to a planned-downtime allowance based on one bad quarter, and next quarter it's back to one failure, I've just given away recurring scheduled hours forever to solve a problem that might've resolved itself. So I want to see it over a longer run — six, eight, twelve months — before I'll treat "three this quarter" as a trend rather than a bad run.

And then separately from that, I'm doing the actual trade-off math that I don't think either side is bringing to me properly, which is: what's the expected cost of the unplanned failures if we do nothing, weighted by how likely it actually is to keep happening at that rate, against the guaranteed cost of the planned hours we'd be handing over. Operations will resist the guaranteed cost because it's guaranteed and it's theirs and it hits their number this quarter. Maintenance will lean on the incident cost because it makes their case, and honestly sometimes they're citing the same shaky downtime-dollar-rate we talked about earlier, so I'm not even fully trusting their number either. I'm the one who has to sit in the middle and put both of those on a comparable footing — same units, same time horizon, same rate assumptions — because left to their own devices, Maintenance will use the biggest defensible incident cost and Operations will focus purely on the guaranteed hours lost, and neither of those two views, taken alone, is actually the answer to "is this a good trade for the site."

So I suppose the honest version is — Operations' instinct is protective of the schedule they're accountable for, Maintenance's instinct is protective of the asset they're accountable for, and my job is to be accountable to neither of those things specifically and just ask whether the trade actually stacks up in dollars, over a long enough window that three bad events don't get treated as gospel. Which, I'll admit, doesn't make me massively popular in either direction some months.

**8. The Maintenance Manager, the Reliability Engineer, and the Mining/Operations Manager all independently told me some version of the same thing: fast fixes keep winning over proper root-cause repairs because production's argument is quantified and maintenance's usually isn't. From where you sit with the actual cost data — is that true, or is there a cost dimension to that pattern that none of them are seeing because they're not the ones who see the full financial picture?**

*(sets the pen down properly now, this is clearly the question she's been waiting to actually get)* Yeah — so, partly true, and partly missing something that I don't think any of the three of them are in a position to see, because none of them sit where I sit.

The part that's true: production's argument is always ready-made. Lost tonnes, times price, times hours — that number exists the moment the line stops, nobody has to go build it, it basically writes itself and it's viscerally easy to understand in a meeting. Whereas a proper root-cause fix argument needs someone to actually assemble a case — engineering time to diagnose it properly, a scope, a quote, a justification for why the cheap fix keeps not sticking. That takes days or weeks to put together, and production's number is sitting on the table in the meeting that same afternoon. So yes, there's a real asymmetry in how fast each argument shows up, and speed wins a lot of arguments that shouldn't be decided on speed.

But here's the bit I don't think Dale, or Kev, or the reliability engineer would actually be able to see from where they sit, because it's genuinely not their number to look at — it's mine. When a quick fix goes in instead of a proper repair, the cost of that decision doesn't show up as one number anywhere. It shows up as five or six separate work orders over the following year, each one under whatever threshold makes it easy to approve without much scrutiny, each one coded slightly differently, sometimes against a different cost centre depending on who raised it, and nobody — not me, not anyone — is rolling those up against the specific asset unless someone deliberately goes looking. I can tell you what maintenance spent on the crusher this month. I cannot easily tell you, without going and doing the work myself, that a particular gearbox has quietly cost the site $380,000 in patch repairs over eighteen months because nobody ever forced the root-cause fix through. That number exists, technically, scattered across a dozen work orders, but it's invisible in any report anyone actually looks at month to month. So there's a real cost dimension there that's genuinely being missed — not because production's argument is quantified and maintenance's isn't, but because maintenance's cumulative argument has literally never been assembled by anyone, including me, because nobody's asked me to pull it that way.

Second thing, and this one's more structural, more about how money moves around this site than about data — a lot of the reason quick fixes keep winning isn't actually about whose argument is more persuasive, it's about which approval pathway each option sits in. A quick patch is usually opex, under my delegation, I can wave it through without much drama. A proper root-cause fix is often bigger dollars, frequently ends up needing to be capex, which means a business case, which means it queues up behind every other capital request on site and might not get a decision for months. So even when maintenance does have a decent number, the honest reason the quick fix wins isn't that production out-argued them — it's that the quick fix is available to approve this week and the proper fix isn't available to approve until next quarter's capital round, if it gets prioritised at all. That's a financial plumbing problem, not an argument-quality problem, and none of the three of them would necessarily see it that way because from where they sit it just looks like "ours doesn't get picked."

So — is it true? As far as it goes, yes. But if I'm being the person who actually sees where the money goes, the bigger issue isn't that maintenance's case is unquantified, it's that the repeat cost of not fixing it properly is unquantified, and it's sitting right there in my own system, just never assembled that way. That's honestly something I could probably help build, if someone actually asked me for it specifically by asset instead of just by month.

But if you strip it right back — one thing, asset-level, cumulative, over time, sitting in front of me before I sign, not after. That's the thing that'd actually change my behaviour, rather than just giving me a better-looking version of the same monthly snapshot I already don't fully trust.

**10. What would make you stop trusting the numbers this project produces, or stop treating them as good enough to put in front of head office?**

*(taps the desk once, decisively, like this is the one question where she already knows exactly what she'd say without having to think about it)* A few things, and probably the fastest way for it to happen is any one of these, not all of them.

First — if I ever ask "where did this number come from" and I don't get a straight answer, or the answer takes three people and a week to chase down. That's it, that's the whole test really. I don't need every number to be perfect, I've made peace with the fact that some of this is estimated. What I can't work with is a number where nobody can walk me back to source in a reasonable time. The moment that happens once, I stop taking the next one at face value, and then I'm re-checking everything, which defeats the entire purpose of doing this project in the first place.

Second — if the methodology moves around without anyone telling me. Same asset, different downtime rate this quarter than last quarter, and nobody flags it, I just happen to notice because the number looks different. I've been burned by exactly that before with the existing reporting, and it's the fastest way to make me distrust a whole series, not just the one figure — because if it moved once without me being told, I have no way of knowing it hasn't moved other times I didn't happen to catch.

Third — and this is a bit of a gut thing but it matters — if the numbers only ever seem to move in the direction that suits whoever's presenting them. If Maintenance's downtime figures always land conveniently high right before a capex round, or if Operations' figures always land conveniently favourable right before a performance review, that pattern doesn't have to be deliberate manipulation for me to notice it and get uneasy. My job is to be the person in the room who isn't trying to win an argument with the number, and if I start to suspect the project's output is shaped by whoever's using it that quarter rather than by what actually happened, I'll stop treating it as evidence and start treating it as advocacy, which is a very different thing to put in front of head office.

Fourth — if it stops reconciling with what's already in my finance system. That's a hard line for me, honestly. If this project's downtime cost or maintenance spend figure for an asset drifts away from what I can independently pull from the ERP and nobody can explain the gap, I'm not going to run two versions of the truth side by side and hope nobody at head office lines them up. I'll default back to my own numbers and treat the project's output as a nice-to-have overlay rather than something I'll actually sign my name to.

And fifth, probably the smallest thing but it'd wear me down fastest over time — false confidence. If every figure comes out looking clean and precise with no acknowledgement that some of it's a genuine estimate, I'll start assuming it's being smoothed over somewhere, because I already know from twenty years of doing this that real operational data is never that tidy. A bit of visible uncertainty actually builds my trust. A complete absence of it does the opposite.

So — traceable, consistent, not visibly serving anyone's agenda, reconciled against what I already own, and honest about what it doesn't know. Any one of those breaks down and I go back to doing what I've always done, which is quietly redoing the work myself before I'll put my name near it. I'd genuinely rather this project succeed at that than not, for what it's worth — I'm just not going to pretend the bar's lower than it is.

**11. Is there anything about how cost, downtime, and investment decisions get made here that I haven't asked about but should have?**

*(sits with it for a second — genuinely thinking, not just filling silence)* Actually, yeah, a couple of things, and neither of them's come up yet because they're not really front of anyone's mind unless you're staring at the P&L.

The first one — commodity price. Every downtime-cost figure we've talked about this whole conversation, that dollar-per-hour rate, ultimately traces back to a production value that's driven by the price we're getting for what we dig up. And that price moves. Sometimes a lot, sometimes inside the same quarter. So if this project builds a downtime cost figure using, say, this month's price, and then in six months somebody pulls that same figure back out to compare against a new incident, and prices have moved twenty per cent in the meantime, you're not comparing like with like anymore, even though the hours lost might be identical. Nobody in Operations or Maintenance tends to think about that, because they're thinking in hours and tonnes, not in what a tonne's actually worth this week. But it's exactly the kind of thing that'll make a trend line look like it's telling you a reliability story when actually half of what moved was the market. If this project's going to build a repeatable downtime rate, I'd want the price assumption dated and locked, or at least disclosed, every time it's used, so nobody's comparing March's dollars to September's dollars and drawing a conclusion about the equipment that's really about the market.

Second thing — and this one's more about scope than method — this whole conversation's leaned pretty heavily on fixed plant, the crusher, the mill, the thickener. But the fleet's a different animal financially, and I don't think that's come up. A chunk of what we spend on the haul trucks and excavators isn't a normal work-order-by-work-order repair cost the way the crusher is — some of it sits inside OEM maintenance contracts, where we're paying a fixed rate per hour regardless of what actually breaks, and the repair cost data you'd pull for those assets doesn't behave the same way at all, because the incentive and the paperwork trail's completely different from something in-house like Dale's team fixes. If this project treats mobile fleet and fixed plant the same way for costing purposes, that's going to produce something that doesn't actually hold up, because they're genuinely not comparable cost structures.

And a smaller third thing, only because you asked — if we ever get to a capital-replacement business case off the back of this, somebody needs to remember there's a depreciation and write-off implication to retiring an asset early that isn't fully depreciated. That's not really a downtime question at all, it's an accounting one, but it changes the net financial case, and it's the kind of thing that gets missed by anyone who isn't looking at the balance sheet, which — again — is basically just me in this building.

None of that's urgent to the core reporting problem you're solving, I don't think, but if this ends up feeding an actual capex decision down the track, all three of those will matter, and better you hear it now than after someone's built the model without them.
