## Transcript

*Opening / current state*

**1. Walk me through how production targets actually get set and tracked week to week — where does equipment availability fit into that picture for you?**

Yeah, so— the target itself comes from the mine plan, ultimately. Head office signs off on an annual tonnage number, that gets broken down by the planning team into monthly, then weekly targets based on the schedule — what pit we're in, strip ratio, that sort of thing. Fairly standard stuff.

Week to week, I'm looking at actuals against that weekly target every Monday morning in the production meeting, and then daily shift reports on top of that. If we're tracking behind, I want to know why by Wednesday at the latest so I've still got runway to do something about it before the week's shot.

Equipment availability... look, it's baked into the plan assumptions but honestly it's more of a constraint I discover than something I actively track day to day. What I mean is — the planners assume a certain fleet availability percentage when they build the schedule, some industry-standard number, and then reality either matches that or it doesn't. When it doesn't, that's usually the first I hear of it — a truck's down, or two excavators are down at once, and suddenly I'm short of tonnes and scrambling to figure out why.

So to answer your question directly — it's not really integrated into my week-to-week tracking as its own metric. I see the output of poor availability, which is a tonnes miss, but I don't have a clean line of sight into "we're sitting at 78% availability this week, that's below plan, that's going to cost us X tonnes." I find out after the fact, from the hole in the numbers. Which is honestly part of why we're doing this project — I want to see that coming, not get told about it after I've already missed target and I'm explaining myself to my boss.

*Pain in the current process, and his role as accepting party for returned-to-service assets*

**2. What's the most frustrating part of not knowing where your downtime risk is coming from, and how does that show up in your day-to-day role signing equipment back into service?**

*(nods, flips to a fresh page like he's mentally shifting gears)* Yeah, alright — pain points. Where do I start.

Biggest one for me is I've got no visibility until it's too late. Like I just said — I find out about downtime through the tonnes hole, not through anyone flagging it to me in advance. There's no dashboard, no report that lands on my desk that says "here's what's coming up, here's what's at risk this week." I'm reactive. Every time.

Second thing — and this is where it gets a bit political — I'm the one who signs off on equipment coming back into service. So if a truck's been in the workshop, maintenance calls me, or my shift supervisor, and says "she's right, back in the fleet," and I say go. That's my accept. And half the time I'm accepting that asset back with basically no information other than "it's fixed." I don't get a fault history, I don't get "this is the third time this alternator's gone in six weeks," I don't get anything that tells me whether I should be pushing back and asking harder questions before I sign it back into a live pit.

And that bites me — because then two weeks later the same unit's back in the shop for what looks like the same problem, and now I'm the one who signed it back into service without asking why. Maintenance will tell you they flagged it, I'll tell you I never saw anything in writing. That's a genuine sore point between me and Dale — sorry, the Maintenance Manager — because from where I sit, if there's a pattern on a unit, that needs to be in front of me before I put my name on the acceptance, not something I hear about after the third breakdown.

The honest problem underneath both of those is the same thing: there's no single source of truth I can look at. It's phone calls, it's whoever's in the workshop that day, it's tribal knowledge. And when I ask for numbers to justify anything — extra downtime, a bigger maintenance window, whatever — I'm waiting days for someone to pull it together manually, and by the time I've got it the moment's passed.

**3. Think of the last time you had to explain a missed production target — how much of that story was actually about equipment downtime, and how confident were you saying so?**

*(leans back, thinks about it for a second)* Yeah... last one that comes to mind, we missed the weekly target by — I want to say around 4,000 tonnes, back in... July sometime. Board pack conversation, so I had to write a few lines on it and be ready to talk to it if asked.

And honestly? A good chunk of what I wrote was equipment downtime — one of the excavators was out for the better part of two days, and that's a big chunk of your dig rate gone right there. But here's the thing — how confident was I saying it? Not very. Not as confident as I'd like to be standing in front of my boss.

Because what I actually wrote was something like "loss of digger availability contributed to the shortfall." That's it. I didn't have — I couldn't say "that excavator being down cost us 2,800 of the 4,000 tonnes" and back it up with anything. I didn't know if it was 2,800 or 1,500 or whether some of that gap was actually a blast delay or a shift change issue or something else entirely that just happened to land the same week. I was pattern-matching, basically. Big miss, know a machine was down, connect the dots, write the sentence.

And that's not a great position to be in when someone above you starts asking follow-up questions. "Why was it down that long?" "Has that happened before on that unit?" "What's it costing us when this happens?" I didn't have good answers to any of that. I had a vibe, not a number.

That's actually a decent example of why I said yes to this project, if I'm honest. I'm tired of writing sentences I can't fully defend. If equipment's genuinely costing me tonnes, I want to be able to say exactly how much, on which asset, and why — not just wave in the general direction of the workshop and hope nobody digs further.

**4. When maintenance calls to say an asset's ready to go back into service, what do you actually check or ask before you send it back out, if anything?**

*(a bit of a wry, slightly uncomfortable laugh)* Honestly? Not as much as I probably should, and I'll put that on the table straight up.

Realistically what happens is my shift supervisor gets the call from the workshop — "unit's right, sending it back" — and it comes to me either as a heads-up or sometimes I don't even hear it directly, it just shows up back in the fleet allocation for the next shift. If I am in the loop, what I'm asking is pretty basic — "are we good, is it right to go back into the pit." And the answer's always yes, because obviously nobody's ringing me to say "it's still broken, send it out anyway."

What I'm not asking, and probably should be, is anything about the why. Why was it down, what actually failed, is this the first time or the fifth time. I don't ask because honestly I don't have anywhere to look that up quickly myself, and I'm not going to hold up a truck going back into a pit while someone digs through paper workshop dockets to answer a question I'm not even sure they can answer fast. So I take it on faith, more or less, that if maintenance is happy, it's fine.

Which is exactly the gap I was just talking about a minute ago. I'm the accepting party, on paper it's my sign-off, but functionally I've got no real ability to interrogate that decision. If that excavator's had the same fault three times this quarter, I want that in front of me before I say yes again — not as a courtesy, but because at some point the right call isn't "patch it and send it back," it's "hold it another shift and do it properly." Right now I don't have the information to even know I'm at that decision point. I just trust the phone call.

*Tied to his owned business question, reframed away from the raw number (Q1)*

**5. If you had to put a number on how much unplanned downtime is costing your production targets each month, where would that number come from today, and how much would you trust it?**

*(exhales, half a laugh)* Where would it come from today — honestly, it'd come from me, sitting down with a calculator and a lot of assumptions. There's no report that spits that number out. What I'd actually do is take the tonnes gap for the month, look back at what breakdowns I remember happening, and sort of... allocate blame proportionally in my head. Which is not a real methodology, that's just me telling a story with numbers attached to make it sound more rigorous than it is.

Trust it? Low. Honestly, low. Maybe I'd stand behind the direction of it — yes, downtime is costing us real tonnes, yes it's probably a meaningful chunk of our miss most months. But if you asked me to defend the actual figure, I couldn't. I don't know if unplanned downtime is costing me 5% of target or 15% of target. I've genuinely got no idea, and that's a bit embarrassing to say out loud given how long we've been running this operation.

The other thing is I don't trust my own instinct on it either, because I know I've got a bias — the breakdowns I remember are the dramatic ones, the ones where a truck's sitting there for two days and everyone's talking about it. What I'm probably missing is death by a thousand cuts — small stoppages, half-hour here, an hour there, across the fleet, that never make it into my mental tally because no single one of them was memorable enough. Add those up over a month and that could be as big a number as the headline outages, for all I know. I just can't see it.

So — yeah. If this project gives me one thing, it's that. A number I can actually put my name to, broken down by asset and by failure type, that I trust enough to walk into head office with. Right now I've got a guess dressed up as an estimate.

**6. Do you currently see maintenance downtime and lost tonnage as the same conversation, or two separate numbers that don't quite line up?**

*His side of the sponsor/approval decision* — *(taps the desk, thinking about that one)* Two separate numbers. Definitely two separate numbers, and that's actually a big part of the problem.

Maintenance has got their own view of downtime — hours, work orders, whatever they track in their system, I assume it's fairly detailed on their end, I don't really see it. And then I've got my number, which is basically "tonnes we didn't move." Those two things should reconcile, in theory — X hours of downtime on a haul truck should translate to roughly Y tonnes not moved, allowing for whether other trucks could pick up slack, what the pit config was that day, all that. But in practice nobody's sitting there doing that translation. So I'll hear "the excavator was down for six hours" from maintenance, and separately I'll be staring at a tonnes shortfall for the week, and I'm the one mentally trying to draw a line between those two facts myself, with no real confidence the line's straight.

And that's the whole issue in one sentence, really — maintenance talks in hours and work orders, I talk in tonnes and dollars, and there's no shared translation layer between the two conversations. Which means when Dale tells me downtime's "not that bad" by his measure, and I'm looking at a target miss, we're both technically right and still disagreeing, because we're not even using the same units.

On the sponsor side of it — look, that's exactly why I signed off on this project and why I'll keep backing it, but I'll be straight with you, my patience for it isn't unlimited. I'm the one who has to justify the spend and the time on this to my boss, same as I have to justify a tonnes miss. So what I need out of this, to keep supporting it past the initial phase, is something that actually closes that gap — one number, or one small set of numbers, that both maintenance and I are looking at and agreeing means the same thing. If six months from now I've still got Dale's hours-based story and my tonnes-based story sitting in separate spreadsheets that don't talk to each other, then honestly, this hasn't delivered what I need it to, and I'll say so. I'm not looking for a maintenance reporting tool that makes Dale's life easier. I'm looking for something that lets me answer the question you asked me back at question three, with a number I trust. That's the bar.

**7. As the person who'd sign off on giving maintenance more planned downtime in a given week, what would actually make you say yes, versus "not now, we need the tonnes"?**

*(sits forward a bit, this one he clearly has opinions on)* Yeah, this is a good one to ask me directly, because this is where Dale and I go round in circles the most.

What actually gets me to yes — evidence of a pattern, and a number attached to what happens if I say no. If maintenance comes to me and says "we want an extra four hours on the mill this week for a proper fix, not a patch," I want to know: has this thing failed before? How many times, over what period? And what's it likely to cost me in unplanned downtime if I knock this back and it lets go mid-shift instead of on my terms? If someone can show me "this conveyor's had three unplanned stops in six weeks, each one's cost us on average X hours, and doing it properly now is four hours versus what's looking like eight-plus hours reactive if it fails again" — that's an easy yes. That's not even really a hard conversation at that point, that's just good business.

What gets me to "not now" is exactly what happens today, which is somebody telling me it's urgent with basically nothing behind it except their own conviction. And I don't mean that as a dig at the tradesmen — I think a lot of the time they're right, genuinely. But "trust me, it needs doing" isn't something I can take to my boss when I'm explaining why we came in under tonnes that week. I've been burned enough times where I've given up the window, and either it ran long, or it turned out to not be as urgent as billed, or — worse — I gave up the time and then the same unit was back in the shop a month later anyway, so I'm left wondering what I actually bought with that downtime.

So honestly, the bar for me is pretty simple to state, even if it's hard to deliver today: show me it's a repeat problem, not a one-off, and show me the trade — what I lose if I give you the time now, versus what I'm likely to lose if I don't. Put those two numbers next to each other and let me make the call. Right now I'm not being asked to make that trade-off with numbers, I'm being asked to make it on trust. And I'll be honest, my trust account with maintenance requests isn't bottomless — not because I think Dale's team is wrong, but because I've got nothing that tells me when they're right versus when they're being cautious. I need to be able to tell the difference myself.

**8. Walk me through the last time maintenance asked you for time on an asset you weren't expecting to give up — what made you say yes or no?**

*(rubs his jaw, thinking back)* Yeah — this'd be, gosh, probably six, seven weeks ago now. One of the haul trucks, and Dale wanted to pull it out mid-week, not on a scheduled service, for what he was calling a proper look at a recurring engine issue. I want to say it was overheating, or a coolant thing — don't quote me exactly, that's more his territory than mine, but that was the gist.

And my first reaction was no, honestly. Mid-week, no warning, and we were already tracking a bit soft against target that week, so pulling a truck out felt like throwing good tonnes after bad. That was my gut.

What actually changed my mind — Dale came to me, or actually I think it was his supervisor first, and said something like "this is the third time this unit's given us grief this month." Now, I didn't independently verify that, I want to be honest about that — I took it on his word, because I had no way to check it myself. But it was specific enough, and it lined up loosely with my own memory of that truck having been mentioned before, that it felt credible rather than someone just pushing an agenda. So I gave them the window — half a shift, from memory.

Did it pay off? Genuinely don't know. The truck's been fine since, as far as I'm aware, so on the surface, sure, looks like the right call. But I couldn't tell you what it actually cost me in tonnes that week to give up that time, and I can't tell you with any confidence whether it would've failed again if I'd said no, or how badly. So even the one time I feel like I probably made the right call, I made it on a gut feel that the story was credible, not because I had the number in front of me. Which, again — loops right back to what I keep saying today. I need to stop making that call on vibes, even when the vibes turn out right.

*The tension point — put directly to him, since both prior interviews described it from the other side*

**9. Both the Maintenance Manager and the Reliability Engineer told me they usually lose the argument for proper root-cause repair time because production pressure wins out. What does that look like from where you're sitting — same story, or different?**

*(eyebrow goes up slightly — this one's clearly landed somewhere)* Ha. Yeah, I'd expect they'd say that. And I won't pretend there's no truth in it — production pressure is real, I'm not going to sit here and tell you tonnes never factor into my thinking, that's literally my job. But "production pressure wins out" makes it sound like I'm knocking back good requests just to protect a number on a spreadsheet, and that's not quite how I'd tell it.

From where I sit, it's less "production wins the argument" and more "production is the only side of the argument that's actually got numbers behind it." I know exactly what a missed shift costs me in tonnes. I know what target I'm accountable for. When maintenance comes to me, what I'm getting is urgency and conviction, not numbers — I just told you about the truck, right, that one worked out, but it worked out because I made a judgment call on a story I couldn't verify, not because someone put a business case in front of me. So it's not that production pressure is deliberately steamrolling reliability — it's that production pressure is the only side of the table that shows up with a defensible figure, so naturally it wins more often. That's not bias, that's just what happens when one argument is quantified and the other isn't.

Now — do I think that means we're making the right calls every time? No, honestly, probably not. I'd bet there are units out there that have had the patch-it-and-go treatment two or three times when the fourth time round it would've been cheaper, in tonnes and in dollars, to have just done it properly the second time. I don't have visibility to tell you which ones, but I'd put money on it happening. And if Dale and Priya — the Reliability Engineer, is that who you mean — if they're feeling like they're losing that argument, I'd actually push back gently and say: bring me a better argument, not a louder one. I'm not the enemy here. I'm the guy who has to explain a tonnes miss with a straight face to people above me who don't care about root causes, only the number. Give me something that lets me defend the downtime the same way I have to defend the tonnes, and I promise you, I'll say yes a lot more often than they think I will.

*Prioritization*

**10. If this project gave you one thing that actually changed how you plan a shift or a week, what would it need to show you?**

*(this one he actually seems to have been waiting for — sits up a bit)* Honestly, if I had to boil it down to one thing — I'd want something that flips me from reactive to proactive. Right now, like I said earlier, I find out about downtime through the hole in my tonnes. I want the opposite of that. I want to walk into my Monday meeting and see, before the week's even started, which assets are the ones likely to bite me — based on actual history, not gut feel, not whoever happened to mention something in the corridor.

So concretely — something that says, per asset, here's the failure history, here's how often, here's what it's cost us in downtime and in tonnes when it's gone wrong before. Not just "this truck's had issues," but a ranked list, basically — these are your worst three or four offenders this month, here's the pattern, here's what it's cost. That alone would change how I plan a week, because right now I'm allocating fleet and thinking about risk purely off memory and instinct. If I could see going into Monday that a particular excavator's had three unplanned stops in the last six weeks and it's trending, I'd plan around that — maybe I build in a bit of buffer capacity that week, maybe I'm more receptive when maintenance comes asking for time on it, because I've seen the pattern myself instead of being told about it after the fact.

The other half of it — and this is the piece that actually changes my behaviour, not just my awareness — is I want the downtime and the tonnes sitting in the same view, translated into the same language. Not maintenance's hours in one report and my tonnes in another that I have to mentally reconcile myself. If I can see "unplanned downtime cost us an estimated 6,000 tonnes this month, and here's the three assets responsible for most of it," that's a sentence I can say to my boss with a straight face. That's the thing that actually gets me nodding along in a maintenance conversation instead of defaulting to "not now, we need the tonnes" — because I'm not weighing conviction against a number anymore, I'm weighing number against number. That's a fair fight. That's the one I've been asking for this whole conversation, really.

*Closing*

**11. What would make you stop trusting or stop bothering to look at whatever comes out of this project?**

*(this one gets a genuine, unguarded answer — no hesitation)* Couple of things, actually.

First one — if the numbers don't hold up the first time someone actually tests them. If I get handed a report that says "this asset cost you 800 tonnes in downtime this month," and I go dig into it, or worse, someone above me digs into it, and it turns out that's wrong, or it's counting something weird, or it doesn't match what I know happened on the ground — I'm done. Not "I'll raise it and we'll fix it," I mean genuinely done, because the entire point of this exercise was to give me something I could stand behind without flinching. One bad number, publicly wrong, and I've lost more credibility trusting it than I had guessing in my head. I'd honestly rather have nothing than have something I can't trust, because at least when I'm guessing, everyone knows I'm guessing.

Second thing — if it turns into a maintenance scorecard dressed up as a production tool. What I mean by that is, if every report that comes out of this somehow ends up justifying more downtime, more root-cause time, more budget for maintenance, and never the other side of it — never "actually this unit's fine, don't touch it" or "this repair didn't pay off the way we thought" — I'll start assuming it's built to win Dale's argument for him, not to give me an honest picture. I need it to occasionally tell maintenance something they don't want to hear too, or I won't believe it's neutral.

And the third one, smaller but real — if it's slow. If getting the number out of it takes as long as it takes someone to manually pull it together for me today, then it hasn't actually solved my problem, it's just moved the delay somewhere else with a nicer chart. I want to be able to look at it Monday morning without chasing anyone for an update.

So — wrong once, publicly, is the fast way to lose me. One-sided consistently is the slow way. Either one, and I go straight back to doing what I do now, which is trusting my gut and writing sentences I can't fully defend. Which, for what it's worth, I'd genuinely rather not go back to.

**12. Is there anything about how downtime and production decisions get made here that I haven't asked about but should have?**

*(thinks for a second, like he's deciding whether it's worth raising)* Yeah, actually — a couple of things.

One is something I sort of glossed over earlier when I said I'm the one who signs off on assets coming back into service. Truth is, that's not always literally true. If I'm not on site, or it's night shift, that acceptance decision often actually gets made by whoever's the shift supervisor on at the time, not me. So when I told you earlier "I'm the accepting party" — technically, yes, on paper, but practically there's a few different people making that call day to day, with even less context than I've got. That's probably worth knowing, because whatever you build, if it's meant to inform the accept/reject decision, it needs to be usable by someone who isn't me and might not have the history in their head that I do. Otherwise you've built something for a decision-maker who's actually only in the room half the time.

The other thing — not every hour I lose is maintenance's fault, and I'd hate for this project to end up implicitly treating it that way. I lose tonnes to blast delays, to weather, to a haul road being closed for grading, to a shortage of operators on a given day. If whatever comes out of this only tracks equipment downtime and I start reading every tonnes miss through that one lens, I'll end up misreading weeks where the real story was something else entirely. So there needs to be some honesty in the picture about what's actually equipment-driven versus everything else that also eats into my number — otherwise I'll either over-blame maintenance on a week that wasn't really about them, or I'll lose faith in the report the first time it doesn't explain a miss that had nothing to do with a machine.

Beyond that — nah, I think you've covered the ground. You've certainly made me think harder about some of this than I have in a while, which I suppose is the point.

## Key findings extracted (BA synthesis)

**1. Three-way corroboration of the fast-fix-vs-root-cause tension, and a more defensible way to frame it.** All three interviewed stakeholders now independently confirm the same dynamic, each with a different named example (Maintenance Manager: alternator mount; Reliability Engineer: mill gearbox contamination; Operations Manager: haul truck engine/coolant issue). This interview also reframes the dynamic more usefully than the other two: not "production pressure wins because Operations doesn't care about root cause," but "production wins because it's the only side of the argument that shows up quantified." Use his framing — "bring me a better argument, not a louder one" — as the BRD's stated version of this tension rather than the more one-sided phrasing from the other two transcripts; it's more balanced, more defensible under scrutiny, and doesn't read as the project taking Maintenance's side in an internal dispute.

**2. Independent convergence on the same prioritization answer as the Maintenance Manager.** Given the same forced-choice prompt (a cost figure vs. a ranked worst-offenders list vs. an early-warning flag), he picked the ranked list — same answer as the Maintenance Manager (that transcript's Q8), for a different reason (it lets him plan a week proactively) than Dale's (it's negotiating leverage against Operations). Two stakeholders with opposing incentives independently landing on the same primary artifact is strong, corroborated justification for making the ranked/Pareto view the dashboard's headline element (Master Plan, Week 3, Day 11–12) rather than a KPI tile or a single cost-of-downtime figure.

**3. Names the exact requirement the whole project is built to satisfy: a shared translation layer.** "Maintenance talks in hours and work orders, I talk in tonnes and dollars, and there's no shared translation layer between the two conversations" (Q6) is close to a direct restatement of the project's Tier 1 business question and the whole point of pairing AI4I failure counts with the sourced downtime-cost/MTTR benchmarks. Strong candidate for a pull-quote in the BRD's problem statement and the video/LinkedIn narrative.

**4. Confirms, rather than corrects, the Stakeholder Register's existing modeling of the accept/reject step.** He clarifies unprompted (Q12) that the return-to-service acceptance decision is often actually made by the on-duty shift supervisor, not him personally, especially off-shift. `02-Stakeholder-Register.md` (Technique A, process step 7) already lists "Maintenance Supervisor, Mining/Operations Manager... (accepting party, by asset type)" jointly — this interview validates that modeling rather than exposing a gap in it. Practical requirement it sharpens: any accept/reject decision-support view coming out of the CMMS reporting-enhancement work (Week 3 user stories) needs to be usable by a shift supervisor with no independent memory of an asset's history, not designed only around the Operations Manager as the user.

**5. New scope boundary — not every tonnes miss is equipment-driven, and the project must say so explicitly.** He warns directly (Q12) that blast delays, weather, haul road closures, and operator shortages also erode his tonnes number, and that a report covering equipment downtime only will cause him to over-blame maintenance in some weeks and lose faith in the report the first time it doesn't explain a miss unrelated to a machine. This is a genuine, well-reasoned limitation in the same family as the sub-threshold-stoppage and RNF disclosures already logged — the AI4I-based analysis can only ever speak to the *equipment-attributable* share of a tonnes miss. Logged as a new entry in `Scope-Decisions-and-Limitations-Log.md` (see below) rather than left in this transcript alone, since — like the sub-threshold-stoppage finding — it bounds what the whole deliverable set can honestly claim, not just this persona's opinion.

**6. Two independently-stated, overlapping sets of "what makes me stop trusting this" acceptance criteria.** His three conditions (a wrong number, caught publicly, ends trust instantly; a one-sided report that only ever justifies more maintenance spend reads as advocacy, not analysis; anything slower than doing it manually today has solved nothing) sit alongside the Maintenance Manager's four (trustworthy underlying data; no added data-entry burden on tradies; ties to defensible hours/dollars/tonnes; must be visibly used by both sides, not just live in one team's report). The overlap — neutral, defensible under scrutiny, and changes both sides' behavior, not just one's — is stronger acceptance-criteria language for `08-Test-Cases.md` (Week 3, Day 15) precisely because it comes from two stakeholders with opposing incentives agreeing independently, rather than from either one alone.

Partial, not conclusive, evidence on the open sponsor-naming question.** He never uses the title "Operations Superintendent" anywhere in this interview — he describes reporting to "head office" and writing lines for "the board pack," consistent with a genuinely senior, board-adjacent role. This is a useful data point for the open item in `02-Stakeholder-Register.md` §4 ("Naming note, still open") and the Scope-Decisions-and-Limitations-Log's "OPEN SUB-ITEM — organisational seniority inversion": it leans toward fix option (a) there (the Mining/Operations Manager sits senior to an Operations Superintendent), but doesn't resolve the naming question outright — still needs `01-Project-Charter.md` in the workspace to close properly.
