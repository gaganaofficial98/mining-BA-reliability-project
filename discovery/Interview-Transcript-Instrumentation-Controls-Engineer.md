## Transcript

**1. Walk me through what condition-monitoring and sensor infrastructure actually exists across the fleet today — what's instrumented, what isn't, and how confident are you in the data quality of what is.**

Right, okay, let me actually go through it properly because it's pretty uneven across the fleet.

Fixed plant — this is where we're strongest. Crusher's got vibration sensors on the main bearings, bearing temp RTDs, and we're monitoring motor current draw. Mill's similar — vibration on the trunnion bearings, temp probes, plus we get oil analysis done quarterly by an external lab, that's not real-time but it's solid data. Conveyors have got misalignment switches and pull-cord trips, a few have belt weightometers, but vibration monitoring on conveyor idlers is basically non-existent — we've got maybe a dozen instrumented out of a few hundred idlers site-wide, and those were only put in because we had a fire from a seized idler a couple years back. Thickener's got level and torque monitoring on the rake mechanism, that one I'd say is decent.

Haul trucks and excavators — this is the weak spot, and I'll be upfront about it. The trucks have OEM telematics — engine parameters, some of it's genuinely good stuff, oil pressure, coolant temp, that sort of thing. But a lot of what people want to call "condition monitoring" on the trucks is really just fault codes coming off the OEM system, and those get thrown for all sorts of reasons that aren't actually mechanical failure — sensor glitches, software updates, a connector that's vibrated loose. I get calls all the time where someone's panicking about a code and it's a $40 connector, not a $400,000 component. Tyres — no real instrumentation beyond what the OEM system gives us, which is patchy. Excavators, similar story, engine and hydraulic system telematics from the OEM, but structural stuff — boom, dipper, that kind of fatigue and wear — that's still visual inspection and greasing schedules, nothing sensor-driven.

Now, on confidence — and this is the bit I actually want to flag before anyone builds a model off this — the fixed plant sensors I'd trust reasonably well, because I calibrate those myself on a schedule and I know the install history. The OEM telematics on mobile fleet, I'd be more cautious. That data comes off someone else's system, I don't control the calibration, I don't always know when a sensor's drifted versus when there's an actual fault, and we've absolutely had periods — I couldn't give you an exact percentage off the top of my head — where a truck's been throwing readings that turned out to be a wiring fault, not a mechanical one, and it sat in the data looking like a real trend for weeks before anyone caught it.

So if the goal is a precursor-pattern or threshold finding, I'd be a lot more comfortable backing something built off the fixed plant vibration and temp data than I would off mobile fleet fault codes without someone cross-checking against maintenance records first.

**2. If we wanted to catch early-warning signals — sensor readings trending toward a known failure threshold before the failure happens — which asset classes could actually support that today, and which couldn't, and why?**

Okay, so splitting it by what could actually support that today versus what couldn't:

Could support it — crusher and mill. Vibration and bearing temp on those are continuous, they're calibrated on a known schedule, and I've got install history so I know which sensors are original and which have been swapped. If a bearing's degrading, you'll see vibration amplitude creep up over days or weeks before it lets go — that's a genuine trend, not noise. Same logic with mill trunnion bearings. I wouldn't say it's perfect — we don't have great historical baselines documented anywhere central, it's more in people's heads and old work orders — but the raw signal quality is there to build something on.

Could partially support it — thickener. Torque on the rake is a decent leading indicator for rake overload or bed compaction issues, that one's fairly reliable. But it's a single failure mode really, so don't expect it to give you broad coverage.

Couldn't support it — conveyor idlers, and I'd put this one loudest. We've got maybe a dozen instrumented out of hundreds site-wide. So even if the sensors we do have showed a beautiful trend toward failure, you're extrapolating that to assets with zero instrumentation. Any "early warning" story for conveyors is going to be mostly inference, not data, and I'd push back hard if someone presented it as sensor-driven when it's really a dozen data points standing in for the whole fleet.

Couldn't support it — haul trucks and excavators, and this is the one that matters most for a downtime story since that's where a lot of your lost hours probably are. Two reasons. First, what looks like a "trend" in the OEM telematics is often not a trend at all — it's fault codes and threshold alarms, which are binary-ish and reactive by design, they're not built to show gradual drift the way a raw vibration signal does. Second, even where you do get a continuous parameter like oil pressure or a temp reading, I can't vouch for calibration drift the way I can with my own instrumentation, because I don't own that hardware or its maintenance schedule — that's the OEM's black box. So you might see a value creeping toward a threshold and have genuinely no way of knowing if that's the engine degrading or a sensor that's twelve months overdue for recalibration. I've seen both happen and they look identical in a spreadsheet.

So bottom line — if you're chasing a precursor-pattern finding for the report, fixed plant bearings is where I'd stake my name on it. Anything you build for mobile fleet needs a caveat attached, and conveyors need basically a "we don't have the instrumentation to claim this" disclaimer rather than a finding.

**3. Is there reliable torque or tool-wear sensor data anywhere in the fleet, or is that mostly something that lives in OEM-embedded systems, manual inspection, or somewhere else entirely?**

Depends what you mean by "tool wear" — because that term covers a couple of really different things on this site, and I want to be precise here rather than let it get lumped together.

If you mean crusher liner wear or mill liner wear — that's not sensored at all. That's manual inspection, someone physically goes in during a shutdown with a measuring tool or does a laser scan if we're being fancy about it, logs it, and that feeds a replacement schedule. No continuous data, no trend line, it's a point-in-time measurement every few weeks or at scheduled outages.

If you mean bucket teeth / GET on the excavators — ground engaging tools — same story, that's visual inspection by the operator or a maintainer, logged manually, sometimes with a photo if someone's being thorough. There's no sensor telling you a tooth is thinning. People have talked about wear-sensing GET products being out there in the industry, but we haven't got any of that installed here.

Torque itself — where I do have real sensor data is the thickener rake torque I mentioned, and there's torque monitoring built into the mill drive as a process parameter, but that's more about detecting a blockage or overload event in the moment than tracking gradual wear.

Now, on the truck and excavator side, there is stuff living in the OEM-embedded systems — engine torque output, hydraulic system pressures — but again, that's telemetry I don't own or calibrate, and it's reported as operating parameters, not wear indicators. The OEM systems don't give us a "this component has X% life left" figure that I'd trust. Some of the newer truck platforms claim to do component health scoring, but honestly that's a vendor black box to me — I couldn't tell you the algorithm behind it, and I'd want to see it validated against actual teardown findings before I built a threshold recommendation off it.

So straight answer: no, there's no reliable continuous torque or tool-wear sensor data anywhere in this fleet that I'd sign off on for a threshold-based finding. What exists is either manual inspection logs — which are periodic, not continuous, and only as good as whoever's doing the inspection that day — or it's sitting inside OEM black boxes I can't independently verify. If someone's planning to build a wear-threshold recommendation, I'd want to know up front which of those two buckets the underlying data's coming from, because the report needs to say that plainly rather than presenting it as sensor-grade.

**4. How often is this instrumentation actually calibrated or validated, and what's your honest confidence that a given reading reflects real physical condition rather than drift, fouling, or a fault in the sensor itself?**

Right, this is the question I'd actually want people to sit with, because it's the difference between a finding that holds up and one that falls apart the first time someone asks "how do you know."

For the fixed plant stuff I own directly — vibration sensors and RTDs on crusher and mill — calibration's meant to be annual for the RTDs, that's fairly standard practice, verify against a reference probe. Vibration sensors themselves don't really "drift" in the same way a temperature probe does, but the mounting does — a bracket that's worked loose or got corrosion on it will absolutely give you a garbage signal that looks like a real trend if you're not watching for it. I try to do a visual and functional check on those during planned shutdowns, so realistically that's more like a few times a year, not a fixed schedule I could hand you a document for. And I'll be honest, it's not airtight — if we're flat out and a shutdown gets compressed, calibration checks are one of the things that can slip because nobody's going to hold the plant down over it.

Oil analysis on the mill — that's quarterly, done externally, and I trust that one reasonably well because it's a third party doing the testing, not our own equipment potentially lying to us.

Thickener torque and level — similar, checked opportunistically, not on a hard calendar.

Now here's the bit I really want on the record: fouling and drift are not hypothetical for me, I've been burned by both. Dust and material buildup on a vibration sensor housing near the crusher can absolutely change the signal characteristics — I've had a reading that looked like early bearing wear turn out to be buildup affecting the mount. And I've had — more than once — a temperature reading trend upward over weeks that everyone got excited about, and it turned out to be a fouled RTD, not the bearing actually heating up. Pulled it, cleaned it, reading dropped straight back to baseline. If I hadn't caught that, that would've gone into someone's report as a real precursor pattern.

So my honest confidence level, if you want a number rather than a vibe — for freshly calibrated fixed-plant sensors, right after a shutdown check, I'd say I'm fairly confident, call it high-80s to 90-something percent that a reading reflects real condition. But that confidence decays the further out you get from the last check, and I genuinely don't have a good way to quantify that decay for you because we're not tracking "time since last calibration" against sensor ID in any system — that's a gap, honestly. Three months after a check I'm a lot less sure than I am the week after.

For mobile fleet OEM sensors — I've got basically no visibility into their calibration schedule at all, that's the OEM's internal process, so I can't give you a confidence number I'd stand behind. That's not me being cagey, it's a genuine blind spot.

So if this is going into a report — please don't present raw threshold numbers as if they're unimpeachable. At minimum I'd want a caveat that says readings are only as reliable as time-since-last-calibration, and right now we don't have the systems in place to tell you that on a per-sensor basis.

**5. If a project like this leaned on sensor data to justify an early-warning or proactive-replacement recommendation, what's the one thing you'd want us to know before we trusted it too far?**

*(pauses for a second, like he's actually deciding which one)* Honestly? It's that we don't have a system that tells us when a sensor was last checked against something real. That's the whole thing. Everything else — the mounting issues, the fouling, the OEM black box on the trucks — all of that I can manage or work around if I know it's happening. What I can't work around is that if you pull six months of vibration or temperature data and build a threshold recommendation off it, there is currently no way — not for me, not for anyone — to look at that dataset and say "yes, this sensor was in good calibrated condition for the whole window" versus "this sensor drifted in month three and nobody caught it till month five."

So the trend you're looking at could be a genuine early-warning signal of a component failing. Or it could be a sensor failing. Those two things can look identical on a chart, and right now, the only thing standing between "real precursor pattern" and "instrumentation fault dressed up as one" is me remembering it, or catching it on a walk-through. That's not a system, that's a bloke's memory, and I'll be the first to say that's not good enough to hang a proactive-replacement decision on — especially if proactive replacement means pulling a component early and eating that cost, or worse, delaying a real replacement because the data said "not yet" and the data was wrong.

So the one thing — before anyone trusts a threshold number from this data — is go find out when that specific sensor was last calibrated or validated, and if the answer's "we don't know" or "it's been a while," treat the finding as a hypothesis to go verify on the ground, not a number to act on. I'd much rather this project recommend fixing that gap — proper calibration logging tied to sensor ID — than have it produce a slick threshold chart that quietly assumes a level of data integrity we don't actually have yet.

## Key findings extracted (BA synthesis)

**1. Confirms a pattern now visible three times: the site's real data gaps concentrate exactly where the project would most like to say something confident.** Fixed plant (High-tier, single points of failure) has the best sensor coverage; mobile fleet (Medium-tier, but the asset class every other interview independently suspects carries the largest share of real downtime — the Maintenance Manager's haul truck final drives, the Reliability Engineer's 5/10-confidence guess about drivetrain, the Operations Manager's excavator/truck examples) has the weakest instrumentation and the least trustworthy calibration story. This echoes the fixed-plant repair-cost data gap (Option B, locked 1 Sept 2026) from a completely different angle and a completely different stakeholder — worth naming explicitly as a recurring theme in the BRD/case study, not treating each gap as an isolated footnote.

**2. Disqualifying, first-hand confirmation of the Q5 framing already adopted.** No reliable continuous torque/tool-wear sensor data exists anywhere on this site that he'd sign off on for a threshold-based finding — liner and GET wear are periodic manual measurements, not sensor-driven, and OEM "component health scoring" is an unvalidated vendor black box. This independently corroborates, from the instrumentation side, what the Reliability Engineer's transcript already flagged from the reliability side (that interview's finding 7: "the actual analysis will run on AI4I's synthetic torque/tool-wear fields, not these specific named components"). Two SMEs, two different vantage points, same conclusion — strong confirmation that the project's existing framing is the honest one: Q5 demonstrates a method on AI4I's real sensor columns, and does not claim this particular site is currently instrumented to run that method in production.

**3. The single most important new finding: there is no calibration-provenance tracking tied to sensor ID anywhere on site, and a genuine precursor trend is currently indistinguishable from sensor drift or fouling.** Backed by two specific, concrete incidents (a vibration reading attributed to dust buildup on a sensor mount, not early bearing wear; a temperature trend that turned out to be a fouled RTD, confirmed by cleaning it and watching the reading return to baseline). His own words — "those two things can look identical on a chart" — are a strong, quotable articulation of exactly the kind of limitation a technically sharp interviewer would respect. This bounds any Q5/Q6 finding and the optional ML module the same way the sub-threshold-stoppage and equipment-attributable-share limitations bound Q1–Q4: a real, structural gap, not a data-cleaning defect to fix. Logged as a new entry in `Scope-Decisions-and-Limitations-Log.md`.

**4. A directly usable second recommendation for the business case: fix the calibration-provenance gap, not just the reporting gap.** His own proposed language — recommend calibration date be logged against sensor ID, so any future trend-based finding can state confidently whether the underlying sensor was in known-good condition for the window being analyzed — is concrete, specific, and pairs naturally with the CMMS reporting-enhancement recommendation as a second, data-governance-focused recommendation rather than a caveat buried in a limitations section.

**5. Independently corroborates the Reliability Engineer's "death by a thousand cuts" conveyor-idler concern, from a completely different angle.** The Reliability Engineer flagged conveyor idlers as his top suspected cost driver by failure count (that transcript, Q4), based on failure frequency. This interview confirms, from the instrumentation side, that there is essentially no sensor visibility into that exact asset class (~12 of "a few hundred" idlers instrumented, installed reactively after a fire, not proactively) — so even if conveyor idlers are the real problem, the site currently has almost no way to see it coming. Two SMEs, two different reasons, same asset independently flagged as a blind spot.

**6. Mobile-fleet OEM telemetry is a genuine trust gap, not merely a lower-resolution data source.** Fault codes are largely reactive/binary rather than gradual-trend signals, and he has no visibility into or control over OEM sensor calibration at all — illustrated with a real-sounding incident where a truck showed what looked like a weeks-long genuine trend that turned out to be a wiring fault. Relevant to how the BRD/README should frame any AI4I-derived precursor or threshold finding applied conceptually to mobile fleet: real deployment there would need this trust gap solved first, even though mobile fleet is where downtime volume most likely concentrates.
