## Entry 1 — Few-shot prompting: User Stories generation (`07-User-Stories.md`)

**Context:** Master Plan Week 3, Day 14 specified few-shot prompting as the technique to practice for this deliverable, precisely because a 17-story backlog (translating BRD FR-01 through FR-13, plus the proposed FR-14, into Jira/Trello-style user stories with acceptance criteria) has no pre-existing house format to draw on — this is the first user-story deliverable in the project. A single instruction like "convert each functional requirement into a user story with acceptance criteria" would have left a dozen formatting and judgment questions to be re-decided independently for every one of the 17 stories: how many acceptance criteria per story, Given/When/Then vs. plain bullets, where the BRD citation goes, and — critically — how to handle a requirement the BRD itself says isn't validated yet (e.g. FR-05, which depends on frontline and CMMS/IT stakeholders no one interviewed). Left undecided per-story, quality and format would have drifted across the set.

**Prompt (the actual instruction followed, reconstructed as executed):**
> "Draft US-01 (FR-01, Maintenance Manager framing) and US-07 (FR-05, planned/unplanned flag) as complete, fully-detailed example user stories, each with: Priority (citing which BRD Must/Should rule branch applies), Traces to (FR → BR), Status (Ready / Blocked — naming the exact BRD Section 17 action or Risk if blocked / Proposed), Story (As a / I can / so that), 3–4 independently testable Given/When/Then acceptance criteria, and a Notes field citing the source transcript and any cross-references. Treat these two as the house-format template — don't loosen the acceptance-criteria testability standard or drop the honest Status field for any of the remaining 15 stories, which must be generated to match this exact structure, sourced strictly from BRD FR-02 through FR-13 and the proposed FR-14."

**Why designed this way:** Two fully worked exemplars communicate a quality and format bar more precisely than a written style guide, especially for a judgment call this project can't afford to get wrong across 17 repetitions: whether to mark a requirement "Ready" when the BRD itself says it isn't validated. US-07 was deliberately chosen as the second exemplar (not a second easy "Ready" story) specifically so the honest-Blocked-status pattern was set as a precedent from the start, not left to be improvised inconsistently on US-08 or US-12 later.

**Output produced:** `07-User-Stories.md` — 17 stories across 8 epics. Three stories (US-07, US-08, US-12) carry an explicit **Blocked** status citing the specific unmet BRD Section 17 action or Risk; one (US-17, the proposed cause-code taxonomy) is labeled **Proposed — not yet in BRD** rather than folded in as if already approved.

**What I changed after reviewing it (substantive, not cosmetic):** before generation began, Claude flagged that FR-14 was still "proposed, not yet in the BRD" (per `Scope-Decisions-and-Limitations-Log.md`, 4 Sept 2026) and asked via AskUserQuestion whether to include a story for it at all. The two defaults an unprompted build would likely have picked — silently omit FR-14 entirely, or include it indistinguishably alongside the 13 approved requirements — were both wrong for different reasons: omitting it loses visibility into real upcoming scope; including it silently overstates what's actually approved. Gagana's decision: include it, but labeled `Proposed — not yet in BRD`, unprioritized, with a note that its acceptance criteria are provisional pending a real BRD v3 requirements conversation. This is a real, documented judgment call that changed the deliverable's structure, not a wording edit.

## Entry 2 — Role/persona prompting + critique loop: Project Charter red-team, round 1 (`01-Project-Charter.md` Appendix A)

**Context:** Master Plan Week 1, Day 1. A charter drafted and then reviewed by its own author rarely catches its own blind spots — the same person who wrote "mobile and fixed-plant equipment" doesn't notice the phrase is unbounded, because they know what they meant. The task called for an adversarial outside read before the charter was treated as final.

**Prompt (verbatim, from `01-Project-Charter.md` Appendix A):**
> "You are a principal Business Analyst with 15+ years in mining and resources engagements, reviewing a junior BA's draft project charter before it goes to a client sponsor for sign-off. You are direct and slightly skeptical — your job is to catch what will bounce back from the sponsor, not to be encouraging. Review the attached charter specifically for: (1) ambiguity — any statement a sponsor could reasonably read two different ways, (2) missing or unmeasurable success criteria — anything not specific, measurable, and time-bound, (3) scope creep risk — any boundary not tightly drawn enough to stop 'while you're at it' requests later. For each issue, quote the exact charter text, state what's wrong, and state what you'd demand before signing off. Do not soften the feedback."

**Why designed this way:** a neutral ask ("please review my charter") reliably produces a neutral pass — generic encouragement with a couple of wording suggestions. Naming a specific skeptical persona with an explicit mandate ("your job is to catch what will bounce back," "do not soften the feedback") changes what the model optimizes for: it stops trying to be helpful-and-agreeable and starts trying to be the reviewer who'd actually block a sign-off. The three named categories (ambiguity / unmeasurable criteria / scope creep) also stopped the critique from becoming a vague "looks good overall, maybe tighten the language" response.

**Output produced:** five concrete, quote-and-fix findings — the success-criteria section described deliverables instead of measurable outcomes; "fixed-plant equipment" didn't name which assets; the dashboard's production-vs-prototype status was undefined; predictive maintenance was a likely scope-creep request that silence didn't actually exclude; and there was no change-control mechanism at all.

**What I changed after reviewing it:** all five findings were accepted and used to rewrite the charter rather than cherry-picked — in particular, the predictive-maintenance scope-creep finding directly shaped the decision (documented in the Master Plan, Section 2) that a predictive-maintenance proof-of-concept would only ever be an optional, clearly-bounded stretch module, never a core deliverable. That framing decision traces back to this single red-team finding.

## Entry 3 — Role/persona prompting + critique loop, re-run after a material rewrite: Charter red-team round 2 (`01-Project-Charter.md` Appendix D)

**Context:** After the six business questions were finalized (a later, separate decision — see `Scope-Decisions-and-Limitations-Log.md`), the charter's Objectives and Success Criteria sections were rewritten around them. Rather than assume the rewrite was automatically clean because the first red-team pass had already happened, the same critique technique was deliberately re-run against only the changed sections.

**Prompt (verbatim, from `01-Project-Charter.md` Appendix D):**
> "You are a principal Business Analyst reviewing a junior's project charter before client sign-off. The Objectives and Success Criteria sections have just been rewritten around six finalized business questions. Red-team specifically for: (1) ambiguity, (2) missing or unmeasurable success criteria, (3) scope-creep risk. Be direct."

**Why designed this way:** re-running the identical persona and criteria against a materially changed section, rather than treating the first pass as a one-time gate the whole document now permanently passes, is the point — a rewrite is a new place for a new ambiguity to hide, and "I already red-teamed this document once" is exactly the kind of false confidence this technique exists to prevent.

**Output produced:** six further findings against the v3 draft, the sharpest being that O5 ("clearly enough to justify a policy") was unfalsifiable as written — any correlation could be argued as "clear enough" after the fact — and that O4 treated RNF as a clean "inherently random" category when the project's own data-quality check had already found 18 rows where that label doesn't behave cleanly.

**What I changed after reviewing it:** O5's success criterion was rewritten to require the statistical bar to be fixed *before* the analysis runs, with a bare correlation explicitly stated as insufficient — this is the direct ancestor of the pre-registered 90% precision / 90% recall bar actually used a month later for the real Q5/Q6 analysis (Entry 11). O4's criterion was rewritten to require disclosing RNF's own labelling limitation rather than presenting the preventable/random split as clean. Both changes came from accepting the critique's specific wording, not a general "tighten this up" edit.

## Entry 4 — Chain-of-thought lifecycle walk-through + a second, independent technique run in parallel: Stakeholder Register (`02-Stakeholder-Register.md`)

**Context:** Master Plan Week 1, Day 2. A stakeholder list produced by directly asking "who are the stakeholders in a mining maintenance project?" tends to be generic — the same ten roles any mining BA template would list, without being grounded in *this* project's actual process or its actual locked business questions. Two different techniques were deliberately run independently and then compared, rather than relying on either alone.

**Prompt (reconstructed as executed, per `02-Stakeholder-Register.md` Section 1 methodology note):** Technique A instructed Claude to first reason step-by-step through the entire breakdown-to-repair work-order lifecycle — failure occurs, is reported, triaged, scheduled, diagnosed, repaired, closed out, reviewed — and only *after* that reasoning was complete, derive every role that touches each step. Technique B, run as a separate pass, asked instead: "for each of the six locked business questions, whose decision changes once this question is answered?" — a decision-driven rather than process-driven lens.

**Why designed this way:** chain-of-thought-before-output forces the model to build the stakeholder list as a consequence of reasoning through the real process, rather than pattern-matching to a generic BA-textbook stakeholder list. Running Technique B independently and *then* comparing the two lists was the real design choice: Technique A over-indexes on frontline operational roles (the people who *do* the work); Technique B over-indexes on analytical/governance roles (the people who *use* the answer, but may never touch a work order). Where a role appeared in both, that was treated as a strong signal of centrality; where a role appeared in only one, that was flagged explicitly rather than silently merged.

**Output produced:** 13 stakeholders, each tagged with which technique(s) surfaced them, plus a two-axis governance model (Accountable Business Owner / Project Sponsor split, resolved separately and cross-referenced).

**What I changed after reviewing it:** Technique B surfaced a role Technique A's pure process walk-through had missed entirely — a stakeholder whose decision changes on one of the six business questions but who never appears anywhere in the breakdown-to-repair workflow itself. That role was added as stakeholder #13 specifically *because* the two-technique comparison caught the gap; a single-technique pass would have shipped a 12-stakeholder register with a real, consequential governance role missing from it.

## Entry 5 — Fresh-context adversarial critique/red-team loop: BRD v1 → v2 (`03-BRD-Maintenance-Reporting.md` Appendix A)

**Context:** Master Plan Week 1, Day 5. The BRD was drafted from six interview transcripts and the charter. A review run by the same context that wrote the document risks confirming its own assumptions rather than genuinely challenging them — so this review was deliberately run in a separate, memory-isolated conversation with no visibility into how the BRD had been produced, the same isolation technique later reused for the Day 9 SQL review (Entry 9).

**Prompt (verbatim, from `03-BRD-Maintenance-Reporting.md` Appendix A):** the reviewer was instructed to act as "a skeptical, experienced Program Manager sitting on the sign-off committee" for the BRD, with no involvement in producing it, and asked to find every reason the document would get bounced back before approval across six categories: ambiguous/untestable requirements, unsupported/overstated claims, missing or conflicting stakeholder needs, traceability gaps, untestable acceptance criteria, and anything reading as AI-generated filler rather than real analysis.

**Why designed this way:** memory isolation matters here specifically because the drafting context has every reason, consciously or not, to read its own BRD charitably — it remembers the reasoning behind each requirement and will tend to fill gaps with that remembered intent rather than flag them as gaps. A fresh context sees only the document a real sign-off committee would see: the words on the page, nothing else. The sixth category (AI-generated filler) was added deliberately to catch a failure mode generic red-team prompts don't check for — language that *sounds* analytical but doesn't actually assert anything checkable.

**Output produced:** 30 numbered findings across the six categories. The single most consequential one, quoted directly from the critique: "FR-06's Must/Should self-contradiction, the missing FR-04/FR-05 traceability for BR-04 and BR-05, and AC-9's untestable 'survives scrutiny' language would each independently be enough to bounce this back before a vote," plus a deeper structural finding — that the document repeatedly cited "independent" multi-stakeholder corroboration as its strongest evidence without ever establishing the personas were actually generated independently of each other.

**What I changed after reviewing it:** all 30 findings were worked through finding-by-finding (documented in Appendix B) rather than selectively addressed, and four specific validation actions were added to Section 17 as open items the BRD cannot reach sign-off without — frontline validation of FR-05/06/09, CMMS/IT system-owner engagement, a Financial Controller-set reconciliation tolerance, and Reliability Engineer confirmation of the FR-07 threshold. These are gaps still genuinely open as of this writing, not closed cosmetically — the BRD's own Section 17 says so.

## Entry 6 — Structured options-analysis: Step 4 process redesign (`04-Process-Maps.md` Section 8)

**Context:** Master Plan Week 2, Day 7, with an explicit instruction: "propose 2–3 to-be redesign options with trade-offs... forces you to make and justify the actual decision." The fast-fix-vs-root-cause fork at Step 4 of the maintenance workflow is exactly the kind of decision a single AI-proposed "best answer" would quietly make on Gagana's behalf if asked for a redesign directly — and the Master Plan's own instruction exists specifically to prevent that.

**Prompt (reconstructed as executed):** Claude was asked to lay out at least three distinct redesign options for Step 4 side by side — not a recommendation, a comparison — each scored against cost/effort, risk, and exactly what pain point it does and doesn't fix, explicitly naming which of the eleven documented pain points no option in the set actually resolves.

**Why designed this way:** forcing a structured comparison table rather than a single answer makes the trade-offs visible before a decision is made, and makes it obvious which problem is being deliberately deferred rather than silently ignored. Explicitly asking "what does none of these fix" surfaced PP10 (the opex/capex approval-pathway mismatch) as a real structural constraint outside a reporting project's ability to solve — a finding a "just pick the best option" prompt would likely have buried.

**Output produced:** three weighted options — A (reporting only, already committed via FR-01), B (a repeat-failure escalation checkpoint requiring joint sign-off), and C (a pre-approved reliability budget pool) — with an explicit statement that none of the three touches PP10.

**What I changed after reviewing it:** the decision itself — adopting Option B for the current phase and explicitly deferring, not rejecting, Option C as a future-phase recommendation pending Finance/capital-planning validation — was Gagana's call, made only after seeing all three laid out rather than accepting a single recommended answer. This is precisely the "options analysis" outcome the Master Plan's instruction was designed to force, and it's documented as a real decision with a dated resolution rather than left as an open menu.

## Entry 7 — Citation-discipline / source-vetting with iterative multi-source triangulation: MTTR and repair-cost benchmark sourcing (`Scope-Decisions-and-Limitations-Log.md`, 25 Aug 2026)

**Context:** AI4I 2020 has no repair-duration or repair-cost field at all — confirmed as a real data gap on 24 Aug 2026 — so the business case's MTTR and repair-cost figures could not be computed from the operational dataset and had to be sourced as genuine external, cited benchmarks. The project's own data-integrity rule forbids a "plausible-sounding round number" standing in for a real citation.

**Prompt (reconstructed as executed, per the Scope-Decisions-and-Limitations-Log entry):** run in a fresh chat (the same fresh-context discipline as the BRD red-team, Entry 5), the instruction asked for real, cited external benchmarks for average repair time and average repair cost per failure event, broken down by failure mode or asset tier where a defensible source actually supported that granularity, with an explicit instruction not to fabricate a source and to disclose plainly if only a single overall figure could be found — followed by roughly 20 searches/fetches using iterative multi-source triangulation: search, fetch the primary document, verify the number is actually stated in that document rather than just restated in a marketing blog referencing it.

**Why designed this way:** the "verify the number is actually in the primary document" step is the one that matters most — a citation-discipline prompt that stops at "find a source that mentions repair time" will happily accept a vendor blog's unsourced "typical" figure. Requiring the primary document to be fetched and checked is what later caught the project rejecting the SMRP Benchmarking Survey and Solomon Associates as sources specifically because they weren't freely verifiable, even though they likely contain exactly the figure needed.

**Output produced:** real, mining-specific repair-time figures for mobile-fleet rope-shovel and LHD equipment classes (Rezaei Dashtaki et al. 2025, *Scientific Reports*; Jakkula, Mandela & Tripathi 2025, *Journal of Engineering Management and Systems Engineering*), a cross-industry proxy for ancillary equipment (Idaho National Laboratory/NUREG compilation), and an honestly disclosed total gap for fixed-plant repair time and repair cost at every tier.

**What I changed after reviewing it:** rather than letting an unverifiable but plausible-sounding figure fill the fixed-plant gap, the decision was to drop repair cost (labor + parts) from the business case entirely, for every tier, and to report the fixed-plant/High tier by frequency only rather than force a dollar figure onto data that doesn't support one. This is a real scope reduction chosen specifically to protect the project's citation standard, not a convenient shortcut.

## Entry 8 — SUBSTANTIVE OVERRIDE #1: ball/rod-mill citation-verification catch (`Scope-Decisions-and-Limitations-Log.md`, 25 Aug 2026)

**Context:** this is one of the two entries the Master Plan's Section 4 specifically calls out as the strongest evidence that Gagana exercises real judgment rather than accepting AI output wholesale — a case where a citation was *real* (not fabricated) but its specific figures were misrepresented, and catching that required an independent verification pass against the primary source, not just trusting that a citation existing meant the number was right.

**Prompt (reconstructed as executed):** Gagana independently re-checked the fixed-plant repair-time claims already in the log against their actual cited source — Table 8 of the Nugroho et al. paper — rather than accepting the earlier summary of that table at face value.

**Why this matters as a distinct technique from Entry 7:** Entry 7 is about vetting *whether a source exists and is verifiable*. This entry is about vetting *whether a claim that already cites a real, verifiable source actually says what it claims to say* — a different and arguably harder failure mode, because a real citation creates false confidence that the number behind it must also be correct.

**Finding:** the "8.7–10.8 hrs for electrical/component failures" range mixed one genuine electrical-failure event (8.7 h) with a non-failure mechanical/demand stoppage (10.8 h, actually caused by "lack of consumer demand," not a failure at all), and omitted a real electrical event (3.9 h) that should have been included. The "46.8 hrs for a process blockage" figure matched neither the real cyclone-clearing event (56.5 h) nor any other single row in the table — it appeared to have conflated that event's description with a different, unrelated event's duration (45.8 h, wear/fatigue-caused). The "~1–2 hrs for shorter incidents" claim and the "no single averaged MTTR is stated" claim were both independently re-verified as accurate.

**What I changed after reviewing it:** the flawed summary figure was not used. Instead, the individually-verified real data points from Table 8 (1.17 h, 1.82 h, 8.7 h, 3.9 h, 56.5 h, 45.8 h; total 137.76 h across 35.7 events, with 3 of 12 rows disclosed as uncomputable due to a timestamp error in the source paper itself) were logged as the usable, honest path forward — though as of the Option B decision (1 Sept 2026), the mill figure ended up not being used in the business case at all, with fixed plant reported by frequency only. The verified data points remain on record in case that decision is revisited. This is the clearest possible evidence of real judgment in this entire project: a citation that looked solid, from a context that had already produced it, was independently re-checked and found to be wrong in a specific, falsifiable way — not a vague "this feels off," but "this exact number doesn't match this exact cell."

## Entry 9 — SUBSTANTIVE OVERRIDE #2: fresh-context adversarial review applied to SQL, catching the OSF Pareto undercount (`Scope-Decisions-and-Limitations-Log.md`, 10 Sept 2026)

**Context:** the same fresh-context adversarial-critique technique used on the BRD (Entry 5) was generalized here from a text document to quantitative SQL work — run as a subagent with no memory of how `core_queries.sql` was built, instructed to act as a skeptical reliability engineer and verify every number directly against the raw data rather than trust the build log's description of what the queries did.

**Prompt (reconstructed as executed):** "Act as a skeptical reliability engineer with no knowledge of how this SQL layer was built. Verify every number in `v_failure_mode_pareto` directly against the raw `readings` table — do not trust the view's own logic or any prior documentation of it. Flag anything that doesn't reconcile."

**Finding:** the view's `classify_cause()` logic picked exactly one "primary cause" per multi-flag failure event using a fixed tie-break order `[TWF, HDF, PWF, OSF, RNF]`. Because OSF sits last in that list, every time it co-occurred with an earlier-listed flag it lost the tie and vanished from the Pareto entirely, even though it genuinely happened. Verified directly against raw data: OSF is actually set on 98 of 330 classified real failures, but the view credited it with only 78 — a 20-event undercount. This was a real-consequence finding, not cosmetic, because Q5 (the torque/tool-wear overstrain threshold question) is specifically about OSF — a chart understating OSF's true rate understates exactly the failure mode the business question depends on.

**What I changed after reviewing it — the override itself:** two fix options were considered. Option 1: add a caveat footnote to the existing single-count view, disclosing the tie-break rule and that OSF's true rate runs higher than shown. Option 2: rework the query to report both numbers side by side, so nothing is hidden behind a footnote. **Gagana rejected Option 1 and chose Option 2**, reasoning (recorded verbatim in substance in the log): a caveat still leaves "a chunk of OSF which actually is causing breakdown" out of the headline number, which isn't a defensible Pareto if a real chunk of a real failure mode is omitted from it — a footnote explaining a wrong number is a weaker fix than a query that isn't wrong in the first place. This is a genuine disagreement with the lower-effort fix an AI-assisted workflow would default to (patch with a disclosure) in favor of the more rigorous one (fix the underlying metric), and it's the second distinct substantive-override candidate in this project, independent of Entry 8 — that one was Gagana catching a citation error by hand; this one is Gagana choosing the more rigorous of two AI-proposed fixes once a real gap was found.

## Entry 10 — Framework-grounded structured output + attribution critique: Benefit Realization Report (`09-Benefit-Realization-Report.md`)

**Context:** Master Plan Week 4, Day 16. A benefits section that jumps straight from "we built a dashboard" to "we saved money," with no causal chain in between, is — per the Master Plan's own framing — the single most common failure mode in real benefits cases, and exactly the kind of unearned claim a skeptical recruiter or finance stakeholder would catch immediately.

**Prompt (reconstructed as executed):** Claude was asked to structure the report against a recognized, named benefits-realization framework rather than an ad hoc list — Ward & Daniel's Benefits Dependency Network — built right-to-left from each charter objective through the chain benefit → business change → enabling change → IT/reporting enabler, and then, as a second and separate pass, to stress-test every claimed benefit in that network against three explicit tests: a mechanism test (is there a real causal chain, or just "better information exists therefore things improve"), a counterfactual test (could this have happened anyway, for an unrelated reason), and an evidence test (is the magnitude backed by real data or an unbenchmarked assumption).

**Why designed this way:** naming a specific, citable framework (Ward, J. & Daniel, E., *Benefits Management: Delivering Value from IS & IT Investments*, Wiley) rather than an unstructured list of benefits is itself a credibility move — a recruiter can check the framework exists and is being applied correctly, not just trust that the benefits sound reasonable. Applying the three-test attribution critique identically to *every* benefit, not selectively to the ones that already looked weak, is what surfaced the report's single largest dollar claim as its weakest-attributed one, rather than letting confirmation bias protect the number that mattered most to the headline story.

**Output produced:** a benefits network scored High/Medium/Low confidence per the three tests, with the Step 4 escalation-checkpoint savings estimate — the report's single largest financial claim — explicitly scored Low confidence: its mechanism depends on human compliance under exactly the production-pressure conditions the project's own discovery found already override sound judgment today, and it has no pilot, no A/B comparison, and no external benchmark for its magnitude.

**What I changed after reviewing it:** the largest dollar figure in the report was not quietly presented as the headline number. It's explicitly flagged, in the report's own words, as "the number most likely to end up on a LinkedIn post or a resume bullet if it isn't handled carefully" — and handled by demoting it to an illustrative, disclosed-as-unbenchmarked figure rather than a confident projection. Independently recomputing the business-case workbook's dollar figures from the same locked benchmarks (and getting an exact cent-for-cent match) before even opening the actual workbook file was also a deliberate extra verification step, not something the framework required.

## Entry 11 — Pre-registered statistical bar, set before testing: the real Q5/Q6 analysis (`EDA-Findings.md`, resolved 1 October 2026)

**Context:** a traceability-matrix cross-check surfaced Gap G5 — `EDA-Findings.md` stated Q5 and Q6 (the torque/tool-wear overstrain threshold question, and whether any failure mode correlates with a specific shift/condition) had been answered, but no record of the actual statistical test existed anywhere in the project. This was a genuine gap between what the documentation claimed and what had actually been done, not a wording issue. Presented with the option to relabel the backlog item as "pending" and move on, Gagana chose instead to run the real analysis (documented via AskUserQuestion: "Run the real analysis now").

**Prompt (reconstructed as executed):** before looking at a single result, a precision/recall bar was fixed in advance — ≥90% precision AND ≥90% recall — as the pre-registered threshold a proposed overstrain/failure-mode rule would have to clear to be reported as "answered: yes." This mirrors, directly, the fix Entry 3 made to Objective O5 a month earlier: "the statistical bar must be defined before the analysis runs... a bare correlation does not qualify."

**Why designed this way:** fixing the bar before seeing the result is the entire point — without it, any result close to "pretty good" invites post-hoc rationalization about what counts as "clear enough." A 90/90 bar decided in advance can't be quietly loosened to 85/85 after the fact just because that's what the data happened to produce.

**Output produced:** Q5 (the OSF torque/tool-wear overstrain threshold rule) **cleared the bar cleanly — 100% precision, 100% recall** (98 true positives, 0 false positives, 0 false negatives). Q6 (whether each of the five failure modes shows a detectable sensor-precursor pattern — not a shift/condition correlation, corrected from an earlier mis-description of this entry) was genuinely mixed: HDF, PWF, and OSF all cleared the bar cleanly (100%/100%, deterministic by the dataset's own generation rules); **TWF did not clear it** — 93.5% recall but only 5.4% precision, failing the precision leg specifically; RNF correctly showed no detectable pattern at all, which is the expected null result for a documented ~0.1% random trigger. This was reported honestly as a mixed/partial result, not smoothed over — a project that only ever reports clean passes invites more skepticism than one that reports exactly where its own pre-registered bar wasn't cleared. A new, previously undiscovered limitation (a cross-sectional-vs-longitudinal data-structure mismatch — the results prove the sensor conditions are distinguishable, not that genuine advance lead time exists) was surfaced as a direct byproduct of running the real test rather than skipping it.

*(Correction, 1 Oct 2026: an earlier draft of this entry misstated these results — swapping which question TWF's partial result belonged to, and describing Q5 as a failure when it was in fact a clean 100%/100% pass. Caught during this project's own final cross-document consistency audit and corrected here against `EDA-Findings.md`, the source of truth for this analysis, rather than left to stand. Documenting the correction rather than silently editing it out is consistent with this project's own error-disclosure convention — see Entries 8 and 9.)*

**What I changed after reviewing it:** the decision not to relabel the gap away, but to spend the time running a real test and reporting a genuine partial failure, is itself the substantive choice here — a result that makes one of the project's own locked business questions look less clean than hoped is exactly the kind of outcome a less rigorous process would be tempted to quietly paper over. It wasn't. This technique is logged separately from the BRD/SQL critique-loop entries because it's a distinct discipline: not "review existing output adversarially," but "commit to a pass/fail bar before any output exists to react to."

## Entry 12 — Chain-of-thought cross-document reconciliation: Traceability Matrix backward-trace and Gap G5 resolution propagation (`10-Traceability-Matrix.xlsx`, 1 October 2026)

**Context:** once the real Q5/Q6 analysis (Entry 11) was run, its result didn't just need to update one file — it had to be reconciled consistently across every document that referenced the gap: `EDA-Findings.md`, the Traceability Matrix's FR-12/FR-13 rows, its BR-05/BR-06 business-objective rows, its own Open Gaps register, `Scope-Decisions-and-Limitations-Log.md`, `Reconciled-Finish-Schedule.md`, and `Efficiency-Tracker.md`. A single-document fix that left five other documents silently stale would recreate the exact kind of cross-document inconsistency the Traceability Matrix exists to catch in the first place.

## Entry 15 — Plan-then-critique loop, explicitly targeting the naive-baseline trap: Module A ML Proof-of-Concept (`12-ML-Proof-of-Concept.md`, 6 October 2026)

**Context:** Master Plan Section 2, Module A's own "How to use Claude" column specifies the technique before specifying the deliverable: "ask Claude to review your evaluation approach *before* you build — specifically to check you're not just optimizing accuracy on an imbalanced dataset." With only 339 of 10,000 rows representing a real failure (3.39%), a model that never predicts a failure at all scores 96.6% accuracy while being completely useless — the exact trap a less disciplined build could fall into by reporting accuracy as the headline number.

**Prompt (executed live, this session, not reconstructed):** before writing a single line of model-fitting code, two checks were run in a fixed order and their results were required to inform what came next, rather than being run as an afterthought: (1) compute and report the naive "always predict no-failure" baseline's precision/recall *first*, so there was a concrete number to beat rather than a hypothetical warning; (2) fit a quick logistic-regression sanity pass — not the committed model — specifically to test whether a plain linear model already struggled in the way the project's own prior Q5/Q6 findings would predict (several failure modes are governed by multiplicative/threshold sensor interactions, not linear relationships). Only after both checks returned real numbers was the committed model (Random Forest, chosen *because* of what step 2 showed, not arbitrarily) built and evaluated.

**Why designed this way:** a prompt that just says "build a classifier and report precision/recall" leaves open exactly the failure mode the Master Plan warns about — accuracy reported as if it means something on a 96.6%-majority-class dataset. Forcing the naive baseline to be computed and stated *before* the real model exists removes the option to quietly skip it once a flattering real-model number is in hand. Making the logistic-regression sanity check's result the stated justification for the model choice, rather than picking Random Forest by default, turns the plan-then-critique loop into an actual decision point with evidence behind it, not a box-ticking exercise.

**Output produced:** the real model (Random Forest, `class_weight='balanced'`) scored 90.2% precision / 43.5% recall at the default threshold — a genuinely mixed result, not a clean win. A documented per-failure-mode breakdown then showed *why*: the model independently rediscovered the same split Q5/Q6 already found by hand-specifying physical threshold rules — strong, mechanistically explainable recall on OSF/PWF/HDF, and a complete blind spot (0% recall) on TWF, the one mode Q5/Q6 had already shown fails a 90/90 precision bar. Two independent methods landing on the same specific weak spot is real corroborating evidence, not a coincidence to gloss over.

**What I changed after reviewing it:** the default 0.5 decision threshold's 43.5% recall was not rounded up or buried — a seven-row operating-point trade-off table (thresholds 0.5 down to 0.1) was built and reported alongside it, explicitly stating that choosing where to operate on that table is a business decision belonging to the Reliability Engineer and Maintenance Manager, not a modeling decision to make unilaterally and hide inside a single reported number. This mirrors the same "the analysis exists, the threshold decision doesn't" discipline already applied to Q5's buffer-zone table and FR-07's escalation threshold — a consistent pattern across the project now, not a one-off.
